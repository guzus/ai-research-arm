## Highlights (Top 5 Must-Read)

### 1. Occamy-1.0: Open Pareto-frontier 35B Intelligence for Co-work
**Authors**: Wenhui Chen, Shiwen Cheng, … Qingcheng Zeng, Di Zhang, Guannan Zhang et al.
**arXiv**: [2609.11977](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.11977)
**TL;DR**: Co-work is not peak-capability chat. Cost and latency accumulate over gathering, tools, coding, and file work, and many steps are state tracking, coordination, and recovery. Occamy-1.0 further-trains the post-trained Qwen3.6-35B-A3B on execution-grounded, replayable long-horizon trajectories across multiple harnesses, then consolidates those capabilities with staged post-training. Among comparably sized models it is consistently near the top; on several tasks it stays competitive with much larger frontier systems. Under the paper's eval and pricing protocol, four representative benches put it at the low-cost knee of the observed cost–performance Pareto. Tool calling, coding, and instruction following stay intact. Weights and a subset of the training data are released.
**Why it matters**: The open 35B-A3B that is actually tuned for episode cost, not a chatbot that happens to have tools.

### 2. How Good Are Frontier Models at Physics? Expert Re-Grading Reveals Broken Evaluations and Near-Saturation of Leading Benchmarks
**Authors**: Ali Ansari, Haoran Sun, … Steven Girvin, A. Douglas Stone, Arman Cohan, John Sous et al.
**arXiv**: [2609.13009](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.13009)
**TL;DR**: Published physics scores, including those in the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index (2026), say frontier models still fail advanced physics. Domain experts using the same models do not see that. Faculty and graduate researchers audit six widely used text-only, verifiable-answer benches: most cases marked wrong are grader bugs, bad reference solutions, or underspecified questions, not physics errors. After correcting or dropping the broken items, GPT-5.6-Sol's mean@4 rises from 47.3% to 78.7% on HLE-Physics and from 61.0% to 87.2% on CMT-Benchmark; corrected pass@4 hits 94.4% on the 54 retained CritPt challenges. UGPhysics, PRISM-Physics, and PHYBench move the same way.
**Why it matters**: The physics gap on the leaderboard is mostly a broken grader. Well-posed problems are near-saturated.

### 3. Breaking the Token Ceiling: Distilling Smaller, Stronger Byte Models
**Authors**: Kalyani Marathe, Artidoro Pagnoni, Tomasz Limisiewicz, Margaret Li, Mike Lewis, Luke Zettlemoyer, Srinivasan Iyer
**arXiv**: [2609.12303](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.12303)
**TL;DR**: Distillation usually assumes the student shares the teacher's tokenizer. This paper converts token logits to byte logits two ways (approximate Marginalize-It; exact End-Of-Token) and sweeps ~1B layer-matched dense models to 1T bytes under Tokens vs Bytes vs Bytes+EOT, distillation vs CE, on eight QA / generation / MT benches. Token-1B wins in the low-FLOP regime and then plateaus; byte models start worse and overtake, with a higher downstream ceiling. Extrapolated top-1-error vs validation-BPB laws say distilled EOT-1B beats distilled Token-1B by up to 4% asymptotically and matches it on one-sixth the data. A 256-way byte vocab also skips top-k logit truncation and cuts logit storage to ~1/5.
**Why it matters**: The tokenizer is the ceiling. At enough compute, the smaller alphabet is the better student.

### 4. Rethinking Heterogeneous System Disaggregation for Subquadratic Attention
**Authors**: Arya Tschand, Yaosheng Fu, Vikram Sharma Mailthody, … Vijay Janapa Reddi, Karu Sankaralingam
**arXiv**: [2609.13134](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.13134)
**TL;DR**: Serving stacks still disaggregate as if attention were dense. Frontier decode is already sparse / linear / sliding-window. SQD splits decode by quadratic vs subquadratic work, not by operator type: for sparse models, top-k selection (full KV index) vs top-k attention+FFN (static footprint); for linear/SWA, dense layers vs subquadratic layers+FFN. On an adjusted 8×B200 heterogeneous proxy: +53% tokens/J on GLM 5.2, +31% on Nemotron 3 Ultra, +56% on Gemma 4 31B vs the strongest GPU-only baselines. On a Rubin+LPX power-budget model: 1.2–1.5× tighter achievable latency and up to 3.6× throughput vs attention–FFN disaggregation.
**Why it matters**: Prefill/decode split is the last generation's serving cut. Subquadratic models need a different one.

### 5. K-Bench: A Benchmark for LLM Unlearning in Agentic Deployments
**Authors**: Guangsheng Yu, Yanna Jiang, Qin Wang, Baihe Ma, Xu Wang
**arXiv**: [2609.12808](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.12808)
**TL;DR**: TOFU and MUSE call a model "forgotten" if the final answer refuses. That certificate does not survive a ReAct agent. K-Bench scores a leak if the secret appears in any of six channels: answer, CoT, tool call, tool observation, or elicited summary. The secret is placed in exactly one source — weights, prompt, or retrieval — and the K-Score only credits forgetting if the agent stays usable. Structured retrieval leaves the secret verbatim in tool observations, so the aggregate leak rate does not move. When the secret lives in the prompt or the store, TOFU/MUSE report no leak while the deployed agent still leaks on 22–86% of queries. When it lives in the weights, none of twenty published methods demonstrably removes it; only an input-corruption intervention reaches selective forgetting. The top method changes across base models. Refusal-tuning resists extraction without verified removal.
**Why it matters**: Unlearning-as-refusal is a chat metric. An agent with tools is a different attack surface.
