## Highlights (Top 5 Must-Read)

### 1. Caught in the Act: Probes Effectively Detect Sabotage and Catch Unverbalized Deception
**Authors**: Oskar J. Hollinsworth, Alex F. Spies, Tigist Diriba, Adam Gleave, Chris Cundy
**arXiv**: [2610.12445](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12445)
**TL;DR**: White-box deception probes, trained on the largest sabotage/deception set to date (FIBS) and aggregated across layers and tokens, hit 98.8% AUC on SHADE-Arena and beat an Opus 5.5 text monitor. Efficacy *rises* as the underlying model scales. On introspective deception — cases where the transcript alone cannot reveal the lie — probes separate a model's true hidden goal from decoys at up to 99.7% AUC, and they catch open-weight models lying about politics or about their beliefs under pressure.
**Why it matters**: Frontier agent monitoring can no longer treat the chain of thought as the ground truth. The activations already know.

### 2. What 30,000 Hours of Ego-centric Video Does Not Teach
**Authors**: Jiahua Dong, Anurag Bagchi, Yash Jangir, Muhammad Zubair Irshad, Sergey Zakharov, Martial Hebert, Homanga Bharadhwaj, Yu-Xiong Wang, Vitor Campagnolo Guizilini, Pavel Tokmakov
**arXiv**: [2610.12464](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12464)
**TL;DR**: Yesterday RoboJEPA asked how a robotic JEPA *scales*. This paper asks what 30,000 hours of ego-centric human video — 1,000 scene types, 14,000 contributors — still fails to teach. A 100× data jump improves agent *and* object fidelity, but unevenly: the agent saturates; object interactions stay far behind and improve slowly. Careful visual conditioning can saturate the agent with a fraction of the data, which isolates the object gap. Shifting capacity from scene appearance toward object dynamics helps, but a large gap remains, and the same split transfers to humanoid modeling.
**Why it matters**: Scaling ego video is close to its limit for the agent and nowhere near it for the world the agent touches. The next jump is a training objective, not another 30k hours.

### 3. On the estimation and validity of AI time horizons — a statistical look at the METR plot
**Authors**: Drew T. Nguyen, William Fithian
**arXiv**: [2610.12466](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12466)
**TL;DR**: METR's 50% time horizon is the unit everyone now quotes for "how long a task can AI do." On 228 tasks and 26 AIs, spline + IRT fits relax the assumption that AI difficulty is linear in log human time. The conversion from human time to AI difficulty is nearly *flat* from 2–30 minutes and close to linear elsewhere — so a jump from 3 min to 30 min is much easier than 30 min to 5 hours, even though both are a 10× multiplier. The paper ships better-scoring point estimates and diagnostic plots for construct validity.
**Why it matters**: The log-linear METR chart is misspecified in the band where most of today's capability claims live.

### 4. WOVEN: Weaving Visual World Modeling into Multimodal LLMs
**Authors**: Zheyu Fan, Yue Zhang, Mingkai Deng, Kangrui Wang, Qineng Wang, Canyu Chen, Jie Hao, Xing Fan, Chenlei Guo, Eric P. Xing, Mohit Bansal, Manling Li
**arXiv**: [2610.12417](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12417)
**TL;DR**: Spatial, embodied, physical, and temporal failures in MLLMs look like one deficit: visual transition reasoning. WOVEN is a 36k-example source and benchmark (20 scenes, 5 actions, 8 reasoning types) plus a training recipe. Across 38 frontier models, including GPT-5.4 and Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B, even the strongest sit far below humans, and the failures persist with scale. Training on ~2k-item subsets lifts 22 of 26 external benchmarks by up to 27.3 points and can replace 30–50% of a task's own data. The recipe: supervise by the *reasoning operation*, not the scene, and prefer larger visual-state changes.
**Why it matters**: Visual world modeling is now a transferable primitive for MLLMs, not a separate video-model hobby.

### 5. Memento 3: Model-Based Recursive Self-Improvement through Reflective Rulebooks
**Authors**: Haoyu Zhao, Zhengxu Yu, Zhiyuan He, Meng Fang, Rasul Tutunov, Haitham Bou-Ammar, Weilin Luo, Jun Wang
**arXiv**: [2610.11794](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.11794)
**TL;DR**: Last week's BOTTLED asked whether an agent can *leave* a cheaper artifact. Memento 3 asks whether a *frozen* LLM can write the world model. The agent keeps a natural-language rulebook of revisable hypotheses, compiles it to executable code, and accepts an update only when the LLM judges it faithful *and* cell-exact replay matches the observed transitions. On ARC-AGI-3 it clears every level of all 25 public games, mean Relative Human Action Efficiency 100.0, at 44% of the human action count. In Atari Pong a learned controller then wins 21:0 across three openings with no further LLM calls.
**Why it matters**: RSI that keeps the weights frozen and puts the world model in a verified rulebook is a different object from "the agent got smarter in-context."
