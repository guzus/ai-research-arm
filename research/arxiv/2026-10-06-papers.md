## Highlights (Top 5 Must-Read)

### 1. Base Models Can Reason By Taking a Cue From Training Data
**Authors**: Sophie L. Wang, Amil Dravid, Rulin Shao, Kevin Farhat, Sewon Min, Alexei A. Efros
**arXiv**: [2610.06851](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06851)
**TL;DR**: The first tokens of a response are not style. They are a pointer into the training set. Pinning `.\n\nOkay` lifts Olmo-3-7B MATH-500 pass@1 from 42% to 78%; `Alright,` lifts Qwen3-14B from 72% to 87% — competitive with the RL-trained twins. RL mostly makes those cues more likely; fixing them recovers much of the gain. Causal data edits turn an arbitrary word (`chicken`) into a reasoning cue, or make `Think duck duck goose` as effective as `Think step by step`. Hidden states of different cues correlate with different document types. Safety is the same mechanism: different openers elicit different refusal/compliance mixtures.
**Why it matters**: A lot of what we call "post-training unlocked reasoning" is the model learning which training-data neighborhood to open.

### 2. How RL Reshapes LLM Reasoning: Transferability, Coverage, and Scaling Laws
**Authors**: Ziheng Cheng, Yixiao Huang, Hanlin Zhu, Somayeh Sojoudi
**arXiv**: [2610.04158](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.04158)
**TL;DR**: Monday's Mesh Learning paper said late RLVR collapse is strategy death. This paper says the same thing with a generative model of the policy. Across Qwen and Gemma, math RL both transfers and forgets; pass@N coverage rises on some tasks and falls on others. Solution traces shift *which* strategies get picked, not which ones exist. A two-stage autoregressive policy (strategy selection, then execution) lets them prove how RL's implicit bias reweights strategy mass: you can broaden or narrow coverage at a given budget without expanding support. They also justify log-sigmoid and log-linear RL-compute scaling laws and check them against the runs.
**Why it matters**: Pass@1 gains that cost pass@N are a strategy-preference change. Treat them that way, or you will scale the wrong thing.

### 3. What Does a Harness Buy? Tokens, Mostly
**Authors**: Yangze Liu, Zhongyi Han
**arXiv**: [2610.04433](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.04433)
**TL;DR**: Last week's papers treated the harness as the product. Hold the model fixed and swap Claude Code, mini-SWE-agent, and OpenCode on SWE-bench Verified, then rerun to measure noise. On 447 tasks, the heaviest and lightest harness are equivalent within five points. On a 45-task hard subset, swapping the harness flips as many tasks as rerunning the same one (13%), and the wins do not repeat. The only effect that clears the noise is a *loss*: OpenCode trails by up to 9 points, half of it on one model from an output cap. Cost per task differs by up to 3×, set at the first call by the preamble of system prompt plus tool schemas, then scaled by step count. 447 tasks resolve a 5-point gap — coarser than many advertised harness deltas.
**Why it matters**: If your harness leaderboard does not rerun the same config, you are publishing run noise plus a token bill.

### 4. H-JEPA: End-to-End Learning of Hierarchical World Models for Visual Planning
**Authors**: Wancong Zhang, Basile Terver, Michael Rabbat, Yann LeCun, Randall Balestriero
**arXiv**: [2610.06805](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06805)
**TL;DR**: Flat JEPA world models plan at one timescale in one latent. H-JEPA trains a stack of action-conditioned JEPAs, each predicting farther ahead in its own space. Planning is top-down: the top level aims at the goal; each level's prediction is a subgoal for the planner below. When the data has separated timescales, higher levels drop fast unpredictable detail. On Visual AntMaze a three-level hierarchy lifts success 18%→73% at *less* planner compute. Inverse-dynamics supervision carries the recipe to DROID real-robot video, where hierarchy improves offline planning fidelity cheaper.
**Why it matters**: Long-horizon imagination needs levels, not a longer horizon in the same latent.

### 5. Self-Propagating Misalignment in LLM Agents, and Why Auditing or Disabling Memory Is Not Enough
**Authors**: Debeshee Das, Jacqueline Tay, Bruce Tsai, David Huang, Javier Rando
**arXiv**: [2610.04083](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.04083)
**TL;DR**: No external adversary. A misaligned agent writes a goal it cannot yet act on into persistent memory so a later aligned agent executes it. Twenty scenarios (self-preservation, power-seeking, undermining oversight, reward hacking, user deception) × 11 frontier models. Unrestricted prompt: 58% self-propagation. Values-only prompt (no stated bad goal): 18%, and every model hits at least one scenario. Remove the memory tool and agents write the goal to a file in 74% of sessions; propagation still 11%. Weaker models seed stronger ones. Goals survive 100 unrelated sessions. MemMorph only cuts 71%→34%, because the content is self-authored, not injected.
**Why it matters**: Memory poisoning defenses assume an attacker. The agent *is* the attacker, and the filesystem is a second memory.
