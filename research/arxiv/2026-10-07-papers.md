## Highlights (Top 5 Must-Read)

### 1. QF3: Fast Flow RL with Filtered Q-Gradients
**Authors**: Chung Min Kim, Brent Yi, Ken Goldberg, Pieter Abbeel, Carmelo Sferrazza, Angjoo Kanazawa
**arXiv**: [2610.08789](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.08789)
**TL;DR**: Flow policies are what you get from demonstrations. RL is how they get better, or how they start at all. QF3 trains a flow with flow matching plus the critic's action gradient, backpropagated through a one-step prediction of the flow's output, and applies that gradient only to action dimensions that stay near the replay action. First off-policy flow RL method to train humanoid locomotion from scratch and transfer zero-shot to hardware. High-throughput off-policy recipe: 10× wall-clock vs FPO++ on locomotion and motion tracking. Same algorithm fine-tunes pretrained flow manipulators on ABC-Sim and Robomimic.
**Why it matters**: Off-policy flow RL that actually stands up a humanoid is the missing half of "we cloned the demo."

### 2. Recurrent Looped Transformer
**Authors**: Yifan Zhang, Jichen Feng, Shihan Qin
**arXiv**: [2610.07591](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07591)
**TL;DR**: State tracking needs an update at every input. A Transformer gives every token the same depth, so the computation path cannot grow with the sequence. RLT splits eight layers into a parallel causal encoder and a recurrent decoder that merges the encoder output with the previous token's final decoder state. Path length grows with the sequence; per-token cost does not. Trained on at most 40 bits, two of five splits hit 100% parity at 256 bits in every seed; the eight-layer Transformer stays at chance. On swap-based S₅ permutation tracking at 8× training length: 97% final-state accuracy vs under 1% for the Transformer, and accuracy *rises* with decoder depth.
**Why it matters**: Recurrence is not a style choice. It is the only way the compute path can track a state the sequence keeps rewriting.

### 3. AdvSim2Real: Training Web Agents Against Adaptive Prompt Injection in a Web World Model
**Authors**: Sarim Hashmi, Mukul Ranjan, Kshitij Mishra, Mikhail Kuznetsov, Praneeth Vepakomma, Nils Lukas
**arXiv**: [2610.08773](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.08773)
**TL;DR**: A web agent cannot ignore the page. The page is also the task. Fixed-injection fine-tunes die to an attacker that adapts after training; ordinary adversarial training dies once the agent solves the (frozen) task. AdvSim2Real co-evolves a curriculum, an injection adversary, and the agent inside a *frozen* web world model. Curriculum is rewarded for tasks the agent solves about half the time. Adversary is rewarded only for a success flip. A 4B agent trained in the simulator gets more capable *and* more robust: completion rises with and without attacks, holds against a frontier-model adversary it never saw, and the capability gain transfers to a real browser. On 150 web tasks, +33.6% relative completion under that unseen adversary.
**Why it matters**: Injection defense that cannot grow new tasks, or cannot grow a new attacker, is a snapshot of last week's webpage.

### 4. Preserving Unstable Modes Through Inverse Dynamics in JEPA World Models
**Authors**: Leonardo F. Toso, Yann LeCun, James Anderson, Oumayma Bounou
**arXiv**: [2610.07540](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07540)
**TL;DR**: Yesterday's H-JEPA stacked timescales so planning could drop fast unpredictable detail. This paper is the failure mode of the representation underneath. Robots have unstable modes: a small perturbation grows unless you correct it. Next-step JEPA plus anti-collapse can *minimize the training loss while collapsing those modes*, so the latent no longer contains the thing you need to stabilize. The fix is an inverse-dynamics term: reconstruct the action from consecutive latents, which forces the encoder to keep the controllable directions. Control-aware JEPA representations then support stabilization that the collapsed ones cannot.
**Why it matters**: A world model that predicts well can still be useless for control if it erased the axes the controller has to push.

### 5. Agent in a Bottle: Can LLM Agents Turn Their Capabilities Into Cheap, Scalable Artifacts?
**Authors**: Ankit Sonthalia, Haritz Puerto, Alexander Rubinstein, Martin Gubri, Seong Joon Oh
**arXiv**: [2610.08775](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.08775)
**TL;DR**: Last week's papers treated the harness as the product. BOTTLED asks whether an agent can *amortize* itself: take a whole unlabelled workload and, under a fixed time/compute/API budget, emit a cheaper artifact (small model, program) that does the job. Ten models, three tasks. Strong zero-shot does not imply strong bottling. 48 of 60 runs fall below the 95% CI of the same model's zero-shot. 31 of 60 lose to the better of two small-model distillation baselines on the same token budget. When it works, it works: Opus 5 keeps ~82% of zero-shot macro-F1 on query-product relevance at ~657× lower reported cost, and ~94% of Jev at a quarter of Jev's projected full-workload cost.
**Why it matters**: "The agent can do the task" is not the same sentence as "the agent can leave a system that does the task."
