## Highlights (Top 5 Must-Read)

### 1. Unbundling Multimodal Vision Encoding
**Authors**: Ellis Brown, Chris Hoang, Shengyi Qian, Shang-Wen Li, Mike Lewis, Marjan Ghazvininejad, Luke Zettlemoyer, Lucas Beyer, Mengye Ren, Jakob Verbeek, Saining Xie, Hu Xu
**arXiv**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17334
**TL;DR**: Vision encoders bundle three choices — a pretrained visual prior, dedicated vision capacity, and pre-fusion placement — that published VLMs never isolate. Pretraining every model from scratch on matched data and compute, the authors find the visual prior is unnecessary: a jointly trained encoder matches a frozen pretrained one. What matters is dedicated vision capacity; an encoder-free model still trails even when its backbone is enlarged by the encoder’s parameter count. That capacity need not sit before fusion: vision-only modules in early backbone layers match a pre-fusion encoder and can cut time-to-first-token.
**Why it matters**: It is a controlled architecture result from the labs that ship the current VLMs, and it says the expensive separate vision-pretrain stage is optional.

### 2. Goal Encoding in LLMs
**Authors**: Arnab Sen Sharma, Eric Todd, Sheridan Feucht, Andy Arditi, Rohit Gandikota, Natalie Shapira, Gabriele Sarti, Constanza Fierro, Lisa Schut, David Bau
**arXiv**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17297
**TL;DR**: In a multi-subtask coding setting, a sparse set of attention heads encodes the next sub-goal; the same heads decode upcoming actions in English (“I’m checking if a word is a palindrome”). The encoding is causal for the next action and plastic: transplanting it into another environment (e.g. a different programming language) triggers the same operation with a different token sequence. The LM also holds the encoding through the action, which a non-recurrent transformer should not be able to do; the proposed non-recurrent goal-seeking (NRGS) account is that it re-reads the spec, compares it with visible progress, and pursues what remains.
**Why it matters**: It localizes a causal, environment-transferable goal representation rather than a surface plan, and explains how transformers sustain goals without recurrence.

### 3. Negative-Only Policy Optimization for One-Sided Verifiable Rewards
**Authors**: Jiacheng Xu, Shuo He, Fuxiang Zhang, Chaojie Wang, Bo An
**arXiv**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17287
**TL;DR**: Many RLVR settings have cheap falsifiers but no reliable positive verifier: a “pass” is only non-falsified and may be a shortcut. NOPO treats verified positives as unlabeled and updates only by suppressing trusted negatives, with a reference-relative negative-suppression loss and a group adapter scaled by the share of non-falsified responses. Across unit-test, constraint-check, and LLM self-verifiers it raises pass@k on code, logic, and math when oracle rewards are unavailable; the gains hold under inference-time scaling and cross-benchmark transfer. NeurIPS 2026 Spotlight.
**Why it matters**: It is the first RLVR method built for the common case where the verifier can only say “wrong,” and it stops the policy from being rewarded for gaming partial checks.

### 4. Classical Energies Learn to Reason 1,000x Faster than Looped Models
**Authors**: Benjamin Hoover, Hugh Blayney, James C. R. Whittington
**arXiv**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17309
**TL;DR**: Looped transformers have become the default “reasoner” on Sudoku-Extreme and Maze-Unique, but they are slow to train and spend many parameters and FLOPs on puzzles that are energy problems. CRAM, a learned Hopfield–Tank energy, solves 99.996% of Sudoku-Extreme and 100% of Maze-Unique after 42 ms and 132 ms of training on one GPU — at least 1,000× faster than looped models — and matches their best Sudoku accuracy at ≥282× fewer inference FLOPs. Energies get built-in early stopping, compute that scales with difficulty, and interpretable constraint weights.
**Why it matters**: It both offers a much cheaper reasoning trainer and is a caution that Sudoku/Maze are too easy to be the scaling test for looped methods.

### 5. CapLite: Prompt-Injection Defense for Small Language Model Agents without Model-Written Security Artifacts
**Authors**: Zimo Xu, Wenzhuo Liu, Hideki Nakayama
**arXiv**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17115
**TL;DR**: CaMeL, Progent, IPIGuard and DRIFT all ask the agent’s own model to write the program, policy, graph or constraint list that code then enforces. Across 15 open-weight models, 13 of them 1.7B–12B, those artifacts admit attacks on 14, 13 and 11 models respectively, and CaMeL programs fail to run in up to 68% of episodes. CapLite instead derives the policy from tool schemas, commits writes before any tool result is read, and admits a write only if every argument that decides whom or what it affects is stated in the user’s request. No attack succeeds in 13,935 attacked AgentDojo episodes; utility under attack is 29.0% vs CaMeL’s 27.2% at 18% of CaMeL’s tokens.
**Why it matters**: It shows that SLM agents cannot be trusted to author their own guardrails, and that a schema-derived write-integrity rule beats model-written artifacts on both security and cost.
