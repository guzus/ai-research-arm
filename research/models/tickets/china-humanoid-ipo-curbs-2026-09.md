Price-and-volume competition without differentiation is the normal end state of
a Chinese hardware boom — it happened in solar, in LED, in EVs, and in each case
the capital markets funded the race to zero margin before anyone intervened.
Screening humanoid listings on "clear technological breakthroughs" is an attempt
to intervene earlier in that cycle, and it is a policy claim about where value
in embodied AI actually sits: in the controller and the model, not in the
actuator count and the unit price.

Whether the screen works is a different question from whether it exists. Listing
guidance that asks a regulator to adjudicate "technological breakthrough" is
asking for a judgement no securities regulator is well-equipped to make, and the
predictable outcome is that issuers learn to present as research companies. The
useful falsifier is concrete: a named humanoid IPO that is withdrawn, delayed or
rejected. None is captured here.

The reporting itself is thin — one outlet, one post, no detail — and the ticket
records that rather than dressing it up. What earns it a slot is the position it
occupies: this ticket set has tracked five Chinese AI-hardware listings reaching
the market in four months, and this is the first sign of the door narrowing.
