      <p><strong>Evidence:</strong> DeepMind's thread calls SynthID Bio "a new family of watermarking methods made for AI-generated biological designs" and claims a "world first" in putting a signature directly into protein sequences "without affecting their biological function" (<a href="https://x.com/GoogleDeepMind/status/2105624656170643854">link</a>). The pitch is biosecurity: the mark works "like a digital ID card" that lets DNA labs verify synthetic proteins and keep sequence databases accurate (<a href="https://x.com/GoogleDeepMind/status/2105624658951176323">link</a>), and the tools are released "on an open basis for research use" (<a href="https://x.com/GoogleDeepMind/status/2105624661912392028">link</a>). @mark_k's summary gives the numbers. In lab tests on three targets, watermarked proteins bound about as well as unmarked ones. A separate AlphaFold 3 version watermarks predicted 3D structures and detects them more than 99.8% of the time at a 0.1% false-positive rate, with negligible loss in prediction accuracy (<a href="https://x.com/mark_k/status/2105615073922138268">link</a>).</p>
      <p><strong>Counter / contradicting:</strong> The strongest objection is the general problem with watermarks: they catch only cooperative users. Anyone with misuse intent will run an unwatermarked open-source design model, or change the sequence until the mark is gone. The relay itself says the work is "still a proof of concept, with resistance to deliberate removal an open challenge." That makes SynthID Bio a provenance tool for legitimate labs, not a barrier against bad actors. Three binding targets is also a small sample for a claim of "no effect on function."</p>
      <p><strong>Context:</strong> The release extends SynthID from images, audio and TTS (most recently in Gemini 3.8's voice models, 23 September) into biology. Its practical value depends on synthesis providers checking for the mark when they screen orders.</p>
