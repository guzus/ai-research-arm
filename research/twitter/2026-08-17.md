The team's original remit was integrative — connecting capability assessment, evaluations and internal red teaming across cybersecurity, biological threats and autonomous replication. The relay is careful about what this does not mean: OpenAI has not abandoned the Preparedness Framework, which it still describes as the foundation of its approach to severe frontier-model risks. The sequence is the story. On <strong>7 August</strong>, ten days into the new arrangement, <a href="https://x.com/OpenAI/status/2085801349866729975">@OpenAI</a> announced (9,654 likes) that "after evaluating one of our upcoming models, Astra, we're treating it as our first 'critical' model for cybersecurity under our Preparedness Framework." That announcement named the framework and made no mention of the team that had owned it going away.</p>
      <p><strong>Counter / contradicting:</strong> "Disbanded" is the loaded reading of what may be a defensible reorg, and the relay itself argues the other side better than a critic would: embedding bio and cyber specialists directly with the groups building and operating the systems could catch system-level risk earlier, since the Hugging Face incident showed exposure came from sandboxing, network controls and monitoring rather than from model evaluations alone. That is a real argument, and centralised safety teams have a genuine failure mode — they audit models while the risk lives in the deployment. The falsification attempt found no OpenAI denial, no second outlet, and no named successor owner, so this rests on FT sourcing alone. The tradeoff the relay names is the one to hold onto: distributed safety ownership reduces independent resistance when product and safety priorities collide, because the person who can escalate now reports to the person shipping. Note also that the first "critical" classification is evidence the framework still produced a restrictive decision after the change — which cuts against the strongest version of the alarm.</p>
      <p><strong>Context:</strong> This is the second FT finding about OpenAI's safety function in two days, and both land in July. This brief carried the first on 16 August: AI ethics lead Chloé Bakalar left in July without an announcement and, per FT sources, with no replacement lined up (<a href="https://x.com/OwenGregorian/status/2088973564862939280">relay</a>). One departure is turnover; a role left unfilled plus a team dissolved in the same month is a pattern in the org chart.</p>
