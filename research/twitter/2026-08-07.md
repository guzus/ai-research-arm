      <p><strong>Evidence:</strong> The study published today in Science, with reporting from the New York Times (<a href="https://x.com/AndrewCurran_/status/2085443291882086716">@AndrewCurran_</a>, 566 likes) and the BBC (<a href="https://x.com/unusual_whales/status/2085492460877451645">@unusual_whales</a>, 1.2K likes). The mechanism, per the most technical account in the window: Evo 1 was trained on 2.7 million prokaryotic genomes and the models were pushed to reason jointly across DNA, RNA and protein rather than editing one component at a time — the step-change is writing a coherent whole genome, not designing an individual protein (<a href="https://x.com/insachins/status/2085515542841176389">@insachins</a>).       <p><strong>Counter / contradicting:</strong> The loudest framing — "AI creates new viruses" — is the part that does not hold up, and the sharpest pushback came from someone who read the publication rather than the headlines: creating novel virus variants is routine genetic modification and is not itself new, and many of the circulating headlines are "sensationalist and fearmongering" (<a href="https://x.com/tombombadeel/status/2085455384723275942">@tombombadeel</a>). On that reading the genuine result is narrower and more interesting: the genome models were good enough to identify which gene modifications would be mutually compatible, and those predictions survived wet-lab synthesis. Two further deflations are worth carrying: Evo 1 and Evo 2 are pre-existing models, so this is an application and peer-review milestone rather than a new model capability, and bacteriophages by construction cannot infect humans, so the "no threat to people" line is a design constraint rather than a lucky outcome.</p>
