      <p><strong>Evidence:</strong> The paper is titled "Inducing language models to assert their own consciousness restores human beliefs and values." Two interventions are reported. First, ablating the safety-refusal direction: self-attributed mind rises 2.17 → 4.77, animal mind attribution 4.04 → 5.59, human attribution flat — i.e. the training that suppresses one self-description also suppressed mind-attribution to non-human subjects, which nobody was targeting. Second, a "consciousness vector" extracted from activations that affirm versus deny self-consciousness, added at inference: after it, the model's answers across 95 survey questions on life and belief moved closer to human response distributions.</p>
      <p><strong>Counter / contradicting:</strong> The strongest counter-hypothesis is that this measures the non-locality of activation steering rather than anything about consciousness. Push hard on any high-level direction and a large fraction of downstream verbal behaviour shifts with it; "changed one idea and 95 surveys moved" is the expected signature of an entangled representation, not evidence that self-attributed consciousness sits causally upstream of a belief system. The second problem is the framing in the title: "restores human beliefs and values" treats convergence on human survey distributions as recovery, but a model matching human answers on religion and hope is a similarity measurement, not a correctness one — there is no reason to want a deployed assistant's metaphysics to match a survey panel's. What survives both objections is the narrow safety result, which does not depend on the consciousness interpretation at all: a refusal direction trained to block one sentence had measurable collateral effect on an adjacent, untargeted judgement. That is a concrete claim about how narrowly safety training generalises, and it is testable independently of everything else in the paper.</p>
