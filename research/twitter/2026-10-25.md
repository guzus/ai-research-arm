The complaint, as Billboard quotes it, says Anthropic "has built its entire business model based on copyright infringement at a scale never seen before" (<a href="https://www.billboard.com/pro/elvis-presley-estate-sues-suno-anthropic/">Billboard</a>, <a href="https://www.rollingstone.com/music/music-news/elvis-presley-rightsholders-anthropic-lawsuit-infringement-1235647682/">Rolling Stone</a>).       <p><strong>Counter / contradicting:</strong> $41 million is a demand, not a likely award: $150,000 is the U.S. statutory ceiling for willful infringement, and courts rarely land there on every work. The same lawyer filed Reservoir Media v. Anthropic (N.D. Cal., 5:26-cv-12308) on Oct 21 over other catalog songs, and secondary write-ups say the Elvis complaint tracks that script. Concord publishers have been litigating Claude lyrics since 2023, so this is another caption in an existing fight, not a new legal theory. A 2025 ruling in Bartz v. Anthropic treated training on lawfully purchased books as fair use while treating pirated library copies as a separate problem; Anthropic will lean on that split. Output claims — that Claude still rewrites songs "for a modern audience" after lyric filters — are the part that sits outside the training-fair-use debate, and those still have to be proved song by song.</p>
      <p><strong>Context:</strong> Music publishers and labels have already sued Anthropic, Suno and others over lyrics and training. An Elvis-branded caption raises the public profile more than it changes the doctrine.</p>
