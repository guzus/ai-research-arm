      <p><strong>Evidence:</strong> Deedy Das's breakdown supplies the legal spine: AI Act Article 50(2) requires generations in all modalities to be machine-detectable, and Article 113 sets application from 2 August 2026 — nine days ago. He adds the technical ceiling most coverage has skipped: statistical next-token watermarking only reaches roughly a 90% true-positive rate at a sub-1% false-positive rate at around 400 tokens, so short passages are effectively undetectable. Gemini already applies SynthID to text output with no public production detector. OpenAI, he notes, had developer watermarking earlier and reportedly shelved it after roughly 30% of surveyed users said they would use the product less. Curran's contribution is the signatory asymmetry — five US labs plus Mistral bound by the Code of Practice, xAI outside it.</p>
      <p><strong>Counter / contradicting:</strong> The strongest counter is that this is not really industry-wide. One reply reasons from incentives: xAI and OpenAI gain share by refusing, every Western lab competes against unwatermarked Chinese open weights, and the EU is a small revenue base — so why absorb the cost? That objection has force on enforcement but not on the record, since the Code of Practice signatures and OpenAI's own support-page language are the labs' documents rather than inference. A sharper technical objection also stands unanswered: a watermark robust enough to survive editing cannot be output-neutral, and nobody has said what happens when the model emits code, where token choice is semantically load-bearing. Treat OpenAI's position as stated intent, not shipped capability.</p>
      <p><strong>Context:</strong> Anthropic's move was read yesterday as a US frontier lab voluntarily going first on provenance. On this evidence the better frame is a compliance deadline that has already passed, with Anthropic the first to say so publicly.</p>
