- [Who Gets to Define the Rules for AI?](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49692118): new since 22:26 — Aidan Gomez / Cohere, dated 13 September 2026 (14/6, posted ~04:50 UTC), sitting just above the 10-point floor. Direct overnight counter to Dario's now-off-page pacing essay, arriving the same morning as the Science bioweapons split. Subhead: "AI Needs Evidenced Standards, Not A Cartel." Load-bearing question: should a handful of market-dominant Silicon Valley labs get to define the safety standards *and* the pace for the entire world, and ask governments for an antitrust waiver so that coordination is lawful? Two historical rhymes: the 1975 SEC NRSRO designation that locked in three issuer-paid bond raters for 25 years and then rated subprime triple-A; the 1985 EU Motor Vehicle Block Exemption that let carmakers set who was qualified to service "safety-critical" machines and took ~25 years to unwind. What he agrees with: independent review of highly capable systems. What he rejects: a small list of labs writing the standard those reviewers apply, then governments requiring every other developer to follow it; risk defined as a function of scale (so only the companies with enormous systems are qualified to judge); a cyber swarm treated as the same surface as a single model; the sentence that coordination would let developers do safety work "without sacrificing commercial advantage"; entry requirements (vast compute, resident evaluator teams with desks and badges, shutdown architecture, deep government relationships) that look like a moat. Four pillars for an alternative: (1) an evidence-based, internationally built risk framework — which harms, which capabilities, under what conditions — funded through public research bodies, binding on what a system can *do* rather than who built it; (2) mandatory transparency (model cards plus serious-incident reporting across the stack); (3) testing scoped by that evidence (cyberattacks, synthetic fraud / voice cloning, manipulation at scale, physical or biochemical weapons, critical infrastructure) — tiered and open to every company, not a designated tier; (4) real assurance — layered like finance / aviation / nuclear, third parties never paid by the party they review, findings reaching the public. July failures, on his read, happened inside the two best-resourced labs with the largest safety teams and a METR arrangement already standing up; a capability threshold or compute limit would not have caught a badly written score and a weak wall around the test. What would: incident reporting, test-time observability, on-prem isolation for anything wired into a hospital or substation, and rules that follow deployment context rather than company size. Close: critical infrastructure cannot be secured by renting national capability from a foreign monopoly; security comes from sovereignty, local deployment, and technological diversity. Newest political / "the waiver is the cartel" thread — the competition-law counterpart to yesterday afternoon's now-off-page Sacks "go ahead" and the morning's Science bio-split.

- [Notes on gotchas while migrating 35kb preprompts from Opus to self-hosted Ollama](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49697014): new since 06:14 — Patrick McCanna, dated 13 September 2026 (18/1, posted ~13:59 UTC). Notes from moving the largest frontier preprompts onto self-hosted Ollama + opencode on a 128 GB AMD Ryzen AI MAX+ 395 (32 GB host, rest inference), aimed at abliterated open-weight ~27B models that will not refuse security testing. Motivation is the Navier–Stokes session-history fight: "the most valuable information isn’t your data — but the metadata about your sessions"; frontier providers are "pirates"; "BYOW." Load-bearing failure: prompts that ran clean on a frontier API founder on a 65k local context window. A 35kb preprompt immediately consumes 14% of that window, then the agent thrashes — repeated tool calls, re-reads of files already read, rewrites of finished work — "briefing a man who is reincarnated every ninety seconds." SOP: split into single-objective prompts; declare opencode agents in `~/.config/opencode/agents` rather than letting Claude Code read a file as a preprompt; set ollama context length explicitly (defaults are "extremely small"); log session state to disk and re-read only the needed slice; cut tool calls per step; replace "don't do X" with "only do Y." MTTF failure signals to watch in logs: identical back-to-back tool calls, multiple reads of the same file, restated objectives, tool-call parse failures, high turn counts relative to file changes.
