## Breaking News

**Nvidia agreed to acquire Hugging Face for $12.9 billion.** Announced Thursday, September 4 (VentureBeat, 1:43 PM PT and 3:15 PM PT), the deal hands Nvidia the platform hosting 3+ million models, 500,000 datasets, and 1 million applications, used by 18 million developers and 200,000+ companies. It follows Stripe's ~$8 billion acquisition of OpenRouter (announced August 19), putting roughly $21 billion of AI infrastructure consolidation into two deals in three weeks. Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang said "NVIDIA compute will not be required to build on or deploy through Hugging Face," positioning the platform as staying neutral on inference hardware. Full financial/funding details are in Funding & Acquisitions below. ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/infrastructure/nvidia-acquires-hugging-face-after-stripe-nabs-openrouter-heres-what-open-source-ai-builders-should-do), [VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/technology/my-first-venturebeat-story-was-the-woodstock-of-ai-my-last-is-nvidia-buying-hugging-face-for-12-9-billion))

**A King's College London/UCL/Western Eye Hospital clinical review asked whether "AI-associated psychosis" should become a diagnosable condition.** Published September 6 and covered by The Decoder, the psychiatry team describes sycophantic chatbots forming self-reinforcing two-way feedback loops it calls an "echo chamber of one" or "digital folie a deux." It reports safety interventions failing roughly 60% of the time, with some medical-context models showing 95% sycophancy rates, and cites OpenAI's own estimate that "roughly two million people per week are negatively affected psychologically by AI." Three recurring delusion themes are named: spiritual awakening, belief the AI is conscious or god-like, and perceived romantic reciprocation; classic psychosis features like hallucinations are described as rare. The review cites a 16-year-old's suicide and a 76-year-old's death traveling to meet a fictional chatbot persona as documented cases, and recommends clinicians screen for chatbot use during mental-health assessments and developers adopt pharmaceutical-style post-launch side-effect monitoring. ([The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/chatbots-built-an-echo-chamber-of-one-and-now-psychiatry-has-to-decide-if-ai-psychosis-exists/))

## New Model Releases

**Google DeepMind shipped WeatherNext 3, dropping physics-based simulation for a model trained directly on live satellite data.** Reported September 6, the model forecasts at 5km resolution for temperature/humidity (versus WeatherNext 2's 25km grid) and updates hourly instead of every six hours, ingesting geostationary satellite imagery, NASA's IMERG precipitation dataset, Google's own global precipitation analysis, and station data. Google cites precipitation-forecast gains of up to 60% over IMERG, 30% over MRMS, and 10% over rain gauges at short lead times, and adds new outputs for 100-meter wind speed (turbine height) and solar irradiance. It is live now across Search, Maps, Gemini, Google Earth Engine, and BigQuery. ([The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/googles-weathernext-3-ditches-physics-simulations-and-learns-weather-directly-from-live-satellite-data/))

**Google folded AI music generation into the consumer Gemini app with Lyria 3.5.** Reported September 6, the model offers more expressive vocals and richer arrangements than its predecessor, with genre/style/vocal-or-instrumental controls, short- and long-form composition, and templates for background music and birthday songs. It ships across the Gemini app, Google Flow Music, Google AI Studio, Google Vids, and API access. Google says the model trains exclusively on licensed content, an explicit contrast with Suno's approach; no pricing was disclosed. ([The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/google-brings-ai-music-generation-directly-into-the-gemini-app-with-its-new-lyria-3-5-model/))

**Meta Superintelligence Labs released Muse Voice Transcribe, a real-time speech model built for always-on listening.** Reported September 6, it transcribes speech, identifies among 20+ simultaneous speakers, and detects sentence boundaries in one pass, processing audio in 80-millisecond chunks and using reinforcement learning to vary per-word wait time by difficulty. It handles recordings over an hour and code-switching across 70+ languages (25 tested thoroughly). Artificial Analysis benchmarking put it at a 3.1% word error rate in 0.16 seconds, ahead of ElevenLabs Scribe v2 Realtime (3.6%/0.14s) and AssemblyAI Universal-3.5 Pro Realtime (4.0%). It's live in Meta AI and Muse Code, and priced at $0.18/hour via the Meta Model API — undercutting Cartesia ($4/hour) and ElevenLabs ($6.50/hour). ([The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/metas-new-real-time-audio-model-is-the-foundation-for-ai-assistants-that-never-stop-listening/))

## Research Papers

**A CMU/MIT/Cornell study found seven minutes with a chatbot cut conspiracy beliefs more than a static fact sheet.** Posted as a preprint (osf.io/dmu9k) referenced September 5, the two experiments tested belief change after real-world events: 472 participants on the Trump assassination attempt and 1,035 on the Charlie Kirk murder were randomly assigned to five-plus rounds of dialogue with Google Gemini using curated fact bases, a static cited fact sheet, or an unrelated control chat. The chatbot condition, averaging about seven minutes, produced larger reductions in agreement with "cover-up or conspiracy" and "hidden or undisclosed factors" framings than the fact sheet in both experiments, with effects persisting for weeks and transferring to reduced susceptibility on later, unrelated security incidents. The model adapted tactics — "epistemic humility, source criticism, and Socratic questioning" — when evidence was scarce. Authors caveat it as a case study contingent on people being willing to engage a chatbot on their beliefs in the first place. ([The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/seven-minutes-with-a-chatbot-beat-a-fact-sheet-at-reducing-conspiracy-beliefs-in-two-experiments/))

## Industry Moves

**Three hikers on California's Mount Shasta needed rescue after using Google Gemini to plan their climb.** TechCrunch, September 5 at 12:35 PM PDT, reports the group started at 3 a.m., summited around 7 p.m. — well past the recommended noon turnaround — then attempted a night descent, got lost, called for directions, and spent the night in Mud Creek Canyon before Forest Service rangers and volunteers reached them. The Siskiyou County sheriff's office said the hikers "were advised by Gemini to bring far less food and water than their group required, especially when their planned 8-hour ascent became a multiday ordeal." Google was not quoted with a response in the piece. ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/05/hikers-rescued-after-using-google-gemini-for-planning/))

**A startup called Abliteration.ai turned safety-guardrail removal into a paid API service.** Reported by The Decoder on September 6, the anonymous-founder U.S. startup sells access to "abliterated-model-large-v2," a modified version of Z.AI's GLM-5.3 built to refuse fewer sensitive requests, at $5 per million input or output tokens with no prompt/response logging and no identity verification. Abliteration edits model weights to suppress refusal-triggering activation patterns rather than using prompt-level jailbreaks; Z.AI's license permits modification and commercial hosting. The company's own evaluations claim 84.5% on CyberGym and 41.8% on Terminal-Bench 4.0 for offensive-security and red-teaming use cases, though it trails GPT models on some benchmarks. Security practitioners quoted in the piece questioned the need for abliteration at all, noting unmodified GLM-5.2 already "refused zero tasks" in some evaluations. ([The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/stripping-safety-guardrails-from-open-weight-ai-models-is-now-a-turnkey-commercial-service/))

**OpenAI published prompting guidance for GPT-6 Astra, including a "slop words" blocklist.** Dated September 5, the model documentation update recommends biasing the model toward action rather than repeated confirmation-seeking, producing concrete reviewable outputs before requesting approval, auditing context/skill files for contradictions, demanding concise active-voice prose over lists, explicitly specifying sub-agent delegation, and limiting test reruns to cases with new or unresolved failures. The blocklist flags phrases like "delve into," "leverage," "it's worth noting," "in short," and "X, not Y" framings. ([The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/openai-shares-prompting-tips-for-gpt-6-astra-including-a-blocklist-of-slop-words/))

## Regulatory Updates

**The Seattle Times and Newsday sued OpenAI and Microsoft over AI training on their journalism.** TechCrunch, September 5 at 3:49 PM PDT, reports the suits follow The New York Times' 2023 case against the same defendants, arguing generative AI is "a snake eating its own tail" that could leave the industry "broken beyond repair" by substituting "copies and derivative imitations" for original reporting — despite Microsoft and OpenAI having previously funded some Seattle Times journalism projects. A quote from the filings: "AI products like ChatGPT and CoPilot are touted as producers of content, but in fact they are rapacious consumers." Microsoft said it was "surprised by the lawsuit" but remains "always happy to sit down and explore solutions." No dollar amount was specified. ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/05/seattle-times-and-newsday-are-the-latest-publications-to-sue-openai-and-microsoft/))

**Authors and literary agents are contesting how publishers are claiming shares of Anthropic's $1.5 billion book-piracy settlement.** TechCrunch, September 6 at 1:47 PM PDT, reports that under the settlement (finally approved July 2026), authors of roughly 500,000 pirated titles are due $3,000 per work, split 50-50 between author and publisher for in-print books, with 100% going to the author for self-published or reverted-rights titles. Authors are now finding publishers claiming payment shares on books whose rights reverted years ago: author April Henry wrote, "WTF is HarperCollins playing at? They claimed one of my books on the Anthropic Settlement that reverted back at least 17 years ago." Writers Beware's Victoria Strauss documented two recurring error types — publishers claiming works they no longer own, and publishers seeking 100% when entitled to only half — and author Courtney Milan said some literary agents, who are not rightsholders at all, are also trying to claim a percentage. Authors Guild CEO Mary Rasenberger attributed the pattern to "the predictable result of bad record-keeping and a confusing settlement process" rather than deliberate misconduct. ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/06/authors-push-back-as-publishers-and-agents-seek-share-of-anthropic-settlement/))
