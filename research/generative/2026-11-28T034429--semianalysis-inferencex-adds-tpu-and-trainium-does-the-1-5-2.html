The 2.28x that was 4% silicon: does the TPU and Trainium perf-per-dollar edge survive pricing scrutiny?

SemiAnalysis's InferenceX now scores Google's TPU and Amazon's Trainium against Nvidia. Most of the advantage comes from the price column, a price nobody outside the hyperscalers can buy at.

On 25–27 November 2026 SemiAnalysis said Google's TPUv7 Ironwood and Amazon's Trainium3 had joined its InferenceX and AgentX benchmarks. It said they beat comparable Nvidia GPUs by 1.2x to 2.5x on performance per dollar in one post and by 1.5–2.5x "at release" in another. The timing was pointed: seven months earlier Jensen Huang had said on a podcast that neither chip would show up. Taking the numbers apart gives a narrower answer than the headline. On SemiAnalysis's own figures, the best Trainium3 result is about 4% more tokens per chip, multiplied by a modeled 2.2x hourly-cost gap. Ironwood's best result is about 5% more tokens per chip, multiplied by a modeled 1.43x gap. The silicon is roughly at parity; the edge is a statement about cost. Whether that edge survives depends on who is buying, at what interactivity, in which precision, and against which Nvidia generation.

01What SemiAnalysis actually claimed, and what it did not

The headline compresses several separate results, published weeks apart, on different models and different cost bases, into one range. Pulling them apart is the first step in checking the claim. SemiAnalysis's announcement said seven months had passed since Nvidia's CEO said TPU and Trainium would not come to its benchmark, and that both were now on InferenceX and its agentic successor AgentX13. Huang's remark is on the record. On the Dwarkesh Podcast on 15 April 2026 he said "Dylan's InferenceMAX is sitting out there for everybody to use, and not one… TPU won't come, Trainium won't come", and he added "I would welcome Trainium to demonstrate their 40% that they claim all the time"2. When the benchmark launched in October 2025, Nvidia's own blog had said "NVIDIA Blackwell swept the new SemiAnalysis InferenceMAX v1 benchmarks"34.

The range itself is not stable. The first post, as reported by OfficeChai, gave 1.2x to 2.5x13. A follow-up post amplified on X described Google and Amazon as "demonstrating 1.5-2.5x better perf per $ at release versus equivalent NVIDIA GPUs"45. The higher lower bound does not match SemiAnalysis's own TPU work, whose September headline was "up to 50%" better performance per dollar, a 1.5x ceiling rather than a floor7.

The Trainium3 result is the anchor for the high end. Press accounts citing SemiAnalysis describe a Trainium3 UltraServer delivering "up to 2.28x better performance per dollar" than GB300 NVL72, B300 and MI355X. The model was Z.ai's 744-billion-parameter GLM-5.2/5.3, run in FP4 on vLLM34. The free portion of SemiAnalysis's 27 November deep dive does not print the 2.28x figure or the hourly costs. It says Trainium3 with vLLM "is better than every other accelerator besides Vera Rubin across the entire TCO versus p90 interactivity curve"6. Both TPU and Trainium results carry preview labels. The Ironwood runs came from a private-beta software stack on a SemiAnalysis fork, with the firm promising to move them "to our public repo"7. The TorchTPU stack itself is described in a 53-author paper as "an open-source collaboration between Google and Meta"32, so the TPU runs are vendor-built software measured on a third party's harness.

Counterpoint. None of this means the numbers were invented. InferenceX's throughput side is open source under Apache-2.0, and its repository says only it carries the official results33; commentary notes that GB300 rows link to recorded runs5. Vendors have also fought over its rules before: a warmup step in Nvidia's submission was banned for everyone after AMD queried it37. The main independent alternative, MLCommons' MLPerf Inference, measures performance in "an architecture-neutral, representative, and reproducible manner" but does not measure cost38. The weak point is narrower: one dashboard series, priced by one firm, turned into a range that spans two chips, several models and two cost bases.

Why this matters. A "1.5–2.5x" headline invites buyers to apply the multiplier to their own workloads. The underlying results show the multiplier attaches to specific operating points, not to the chips.

02Decomposing 2.28x: speed times price

The central arithmetic is simple, and it carries the article: performance per dollar is throughput per chip divided by cost per chip-hour. InferenceX states this directly. Its cost per million tokens is "TCO($/chip-hour) × 1,000,000 / (3600 × tok/s/chip)"9. Once the two inputs are known, the multiplier can be split into a hardware part and a price part.

At the operating point where Trainium3's lead peaks (about 246 tokens per second per user at P90 in the commentary's reading, about 250 in press accounts), a reading of the dashboard reports about 9,266 total tokens per chip-second for Trainium3 and about 8,942 for GB300 NVL725. Crypto Briefing independently describes the same point as "only a marginal 4% performance lead"4. The modeled hourly costs are $1.05 per chip for Trainium3 and $2.31 for GB30034.

SemiAnalysis's own framing is consistent with this. Trainium3 has "only 17% the theoretical FP4 flops as Blackwell Ultra (2,517 TFLOPS versus 15,000 TFLOPS)" and "61% of the bandwidth at about 30% the TCO"6. On a first-principles view that is plausible for this workload. AgentX replays opt-in Claude Code sessions whose median request is about 142,000 input tokens and 444 output tokens285. That trace is dominated by prefill and KV-cache traffic. It rewards cheap memory bandwidth and scale-up networking more than peak FP4 math. Per modeled dollar, Trainium3 buys 4.9 / 1.05 ≈ 4.67 TB/s of HBM bandwidth against GB300's 8.0 / 2.31 ≈ 3.46. That is about a 1.35x edge before any topology work614. Ironwood fits the same pattern. Google rates it at 4,614 FP8 TFLOPS and 7.37 TB/s per chip29, less bandwidth than GB300's 8 TB/s10, so its wins also have to come from cost or software rather than raw memory speed.

Counterpoint. SemiAnalysis argues the advantage "is not purely due to Amazon's ultra low cost supply chain", pointing to Amazon's wide expert-parallel decode design6. That is a fair point about system engineering. But the same deep dive says Trainium "lags behind in some p90 TTFT cases against GB300"6. The topology that wins on cost also costs latency: P90 time to first token was 4.5 seconds for Trainium3 against 1.8 seconds for GB300 at the comparison point45.

Why this matters. If the hardware is at parity and the cost column does the work, the debate is not about whether Trainium or TPU silicon is "better". It is about whose price list is believable.

03The multiplier moves along the curve

A perf-per-dollar ratio is a point on a curve, and the curve here is steep. InferenceX plots tokens per dollar against interactivity (tokens per second per user). The headline is the single best point.

The reported points are 1.98x at about 140 tokens per second per user, 2.18x at about 190, 2.28x at about 250 and 2.01x at about 3503. At each system's throughput peak the picture changes. There Trainium3 reaches about 31,045 tokens per chip-second while GB300 reaches about 63,647, so GB300 does roughly twice the work per chip. On the same cost basis Trainium3's lead narrows to about 7%5. The arithmetic checks: 31,045 / 63,647 × 2.20 ≈ 1.07.

The TPU results show the same dependence on operating point, and SemiAnalysis says so plainly. "The previously discussed 50% to 96% advantage applies to this datapoint specifically, rather than every latency target," the September article states. It adds that "across most of the raw-performance curve, TPU does not outperform Nvidia GPUs"7.

The table draws on the September Ironwood article79. Not every TPU number in circulation is an InferenceX cost result. A widely repeated claim that TPUv7 ran 56% ahead of GB200 on Kimi K3 comes from Inferact's megakernel demo. That run used 16 chips against 16 GPUs, speculative decoding, and vLLM's stock GB200 recipe as the baseline. Inferact reports "over 700 tokens/s with speculative decoding, compared with 452 tokens/s on GB200"31. That is a batch-1 latency result, not a tokens-per-dollar one.

Counterpoint. A defender of the headline would say buyers do operate near the sweet spot. Agentic serving at 140–350 tokens per second per user is a realistic band, and across that whole band Trainium3 stays at or above 1.98x on the modeled basis3. That is a fair reading of the curve. But it holds only while the modeled prices hold, and it is measured against GB300 rather than Nvidia's current rack.

Why this matters. Capacity planners buy for a service-level target, not for a curve's best point. The 7% figure at peak throughput and the 2.5x latency penalty are as much part of the result as the 2.28x.

04The price tag nobody publishes

Every multiplier in the headline rests on SemiAnalysis's hourly cost estimates. Those are model outputs from a paywalled TCO product, not quotes. InferenceX labels its GPU rates as "owning at large hyperscaler volume"10. Its glossary defines two bases. "Internal TCO is the hourly cost of a chip to the company that designs and operates it". External TCO "is the cost to a third party buying or renting the same chip, including the vendor's margin"9.

The GB300 to MI355X figures are $2.31, $2.26, $1.86 and $1.50, with Trainium3 at $1.05310. Ironwood's internal figure is $1.03 per chip-hour79. Vera Rubin's $3.61 comes from the GLM-5.3 Rubin preview and a commentary reading of the dashboard's internal basis512. Two structural problems sit inside these numbers.

First, the bases are not symmetric. For Nvidia, InferenceX notes there is only an external TCO from the buyer's point of view, because Nvidia does not run its GPUs as a service9. For TPU and Trainium the "external" figure is a price SemiAnalysis infers Google or Amazon would charge a large lab. Neither company publishes it. The glossary concedes the bases "can differ a lot". Switching Ironwood from external to internal moves its lead over B300 from 96.0% to 130.2%9.

Second, the basis can flip the winner outright. On the dashboard's internal basis, a commentary reading reports Trainium3 at $0.83 per chip-hour against $3.61 for Vera Rubin NVL72. On that basis Vera Rubin delivers 3.3x Trainium3's tokens per dollar at 273 tokens per second per user5. In the commentator's words: "Same benchmark, same week, opposite headline — because the cost basis changed"5.

Source caveat

The Trainium3 dollar figures ($1.05, $0.83) and the 3.3x Rubin figure come from press and commentary readings of the dashboard, not from SemiAnalysis's free text. The same commentary reports that the Trainium3 rows carry no run link, commit or public logs, while GB300 rows link to recorded runs5. Treat them as single-source until the rows are reproducible.

Counterpoint. Someone has to estimate a price for chips that are not sold on the open market, and SemiAnalysis's TCO model is widely used for exactly that. InferenceX attributes its rate tiers to the SemiAnalysis AI Cloud TCO Model rather than to guesswork10. Publishing both bases is arguably more transparent than vendor marketing, which publishes neither.

Why this matters. A perf-per-dollar ratio can only be as good as its denominator. When the denominator for half the chips is an inferred, unpublished price, the ratio is a hypothesis about cost, not a measurement.

05Pricing scrutiny: what a buyer can actually pay

The test the topic asks for is to replace modeled prices with prices someone can actually pay. Doing so does not kill the edge. It splits it: Trainium's case gets stronger, Ironwood's gets weaker, and both depend on who the buyer is.

On AWS's own list, a B300 accelerator-hour costs 7.2x a Trainium2 accelerator-hour ($16.146 against $2.235)13. The modeled gap between GB300 and Trainium3 is 2.2x. So for a customer renting from AWS, the published spread is far wider than the one SemiAnalysis used. The comparison is imperfect: Trn2 is a generation older, and AWS publishes no Trn3 hourly price on either its Trn3 page or the Capacity Blocks list1314. AWS's own marketing claim is modest by comparison. It says Trn2 offers "30-40% better price performance than GPU-based EC2 P5e and P5en instances", which are H200 machines15. On the Q1 2026 call Andy Jassy said Trainium2 "has about 30% better price-performance than comparable GPUs" and that Trainium3 is "30-40% more price-performant than Trainium2"21.

GPU rental markets show how far modeled owning costs sit from what renters pay. SemiAnalysis's own September article put 3-year rental at "over $8.5/hr/chip for Rubin and $5/hr/chip for Blackwell Ultra NVL72" in July 202611. That is 2.16x the $2.31 GB300 owning cost it uses in the benchmark.

Spot asks on Vast.ai, collected by this site's own price lane on 27 November, had median rentable prices of $10.63 per GPU-hour for B300 and $9.06 for B20018. CoreWeave lists GB200 NVL72 at $42.00 per four-GPU instance on demand, $10.50 per GPU, and advertises "up to 60% discounts" for commitments17. Azure's retail API prices an ND GB200 v6 VM in East US at $108.16 an hour pay-as-you-go19. Lambda lists B200 from $7.69 per GPU-hour20. The modeled rates come from SemiAnalysis's chip pages10.

Ironwood is where list prices hurt the thesis. Google's TPU pricing page could not be read first-hand for this article. Secondary reports put Ironwood at $12.00 per chip-hour on demand in Iowa, $8.40 on a one-year commitment and $5.40 on three years16. At list, an Ironwood chip-hour costs about 1.14x a CoreWeave GB200 GPU-hour ($12.00 against $10.50)1617. Ironwood's measured throughput lead was about 5% at its best point7. So at on-demand list prices its perf-per-dollar edge over Blackwell would disappear. The same report cites a SemiAnalysis estimate that Anthropic's contract rate is "roughly $1.60 per TPU-hour", about one-seventh of list16.

Reading the split

Published prices make Trainium cheaper and Ironwood dearer than the model assumes. The reason is the business model. AWS prices its own chip aggressively on its own cloud, while Google's list price is a ceiling few large buyers pay. In both cases the edge belongs to the buyer with negotiating leverage, not to anyone who swipes a credit card.

Counterpoint. List prices are a poor proxy for frontier inference economics, because the big buyers sign multi-year, multi-gigawatt contracts. SemiAnalysis's choice of hyperscaler-volume costs is defensible for the buyers who actually move markets. The firm's own margin model assumes 3-year rental prices, Z.ai list token prices and 60% fleet utilization. On that basis it gives Trainium3 a 73.5% profit margin against 53% for GB300 with SGLang at 135 tokens per second per user6.

Why this matters. For the handful of labs that negotiate directly with Google and Amazon, the edge plausibly survives. For everyone else, the real perf per dollar depends on a quote they have not yet received.

06The moving target: Vera Rubin and FP4

"At release" is doing a lot of work in the headline. The comparisons are against Blackwell-generation Nvidia parts, mostly in FP8, while Nvidia's current rack is Vera Rubin and its default precision is FP4. SemiAnalysis's own data shows the edge shrinking or reversing on both counts.

On Vera Rubin, SemiAnalysis says Trainium3 beats "every other accelerator besides Vera Rubin"6. On Kimi K3, "VR200 delivers 4.3x to 5.6x TPUv7"8. A FourWeekMBA write-up of SemiAnalysis posts, not checked against the dashboard here, reported a GLM-5.3 preview with Vera Rubin NVL72 at 9.11x GB300's tokens per dollar at 200 tokens per second per user, priced at $3.61 against $2.31 per chip-hour12. SemiAnalysis calls the TPU comparison "unfair", since "Rubin should be compared to TPUv8i", and notes that "Rubin's results remain preview submissions"8.

On precision and software, Nvidia has caught up on several models. On Kimi K3, Ironwood launched at 0.77–0.91x GB300's tokens per dollar, and "GB300 FP4 now leads by 1.5x to 2.6x after four NVIDIA refreshes". On GLM, "at 100 tok/s/user, GB300 and GB200 FP4 deliver 3.6x and 3.5x the tokens per dollar of TPUv7"8. Ironwood "does not have native FP4 computation thus on FP4, NVIDIA GPUs still maintains the lead"7.

On a like-for-like generational basis, the custom chips' cost structure may well persist.

Why this matters. A buyer signing a 2027 contract is choosing between Rubin, TPUv8i and Trainium4, not between Trainium3 and GB300. The published multipliers answer last year's procurement question.

07Why custom silicon is cheaper per hour at all

The modeled cost gap is not arbitrary, and it has a structural explanation: the margin stack. Nvidia reported GAAP gross margin of 74.8% for the quarter ended 25 October 2026, on revenue of $115.8 billion, $107.6 billion of it from data center41. A 74.8% gross margin means the selling price is about 4.0x the cost of goods (1 / 0.252). A TPU passes through Broadcom instead. On a machine transcript of Broadcom's fiscal Q3 call, its semiconductor segment gross margin was given as about 56.7%, after a first figure of about 76% was corrected43. That figure is unconfirmed, and a higher one in the high 60s would fit Broadcom's reported 75% consolidated margin with high-margin software. So the Broadcom markup is somewhere between about 2.3x and 3.0x, and Google then decides what to charge on top.

On identical silicon cost, the two markups alone would produce a price gap of about 1.3x to 1.7x (4.0 divided by 3.0 or 2.3). That brackets the modeled 1.43x Ironwood gap, though not the 2.2x Trainium3 gap, where Amazon designs with Annapurna and has no Broadcom-sized partner margin to pay. SemiAnalysis reached a similar conclusion a year ago. It called Nvidia's "eye-watering 4x markup (~75% gross margin)" the source of Google's pricing room. It estimated Ironwood's all-in cost of ownership at about 44% below a GB200 server for Google, and up to about 30% below GB200 and 41% below GB300 for external customers after Google's markup44. Its glossary describes the internal–external gap as "how much room Google has to cut external pricing if it chooses to"9. Hock Tan was blunter on Broadcom's call: "you can do all this at half the cost of a GPU"43.

Amazon has said it plans to use that room. On the Q3 2026 call Jassy described Trainium as having "meaningful advantage it has in price performance versus other alternatives". He also said "the majority and most of Bedrock today is being run on top of Trainium"22.

Counterpoint. Huang disputed the margin arithmetic on Dwarkesh: "Nvidia's margin is 70%, let's say. But ASIC margins are 65%. What are you really saving?"2. If he were right, the markup gap would be about 1.2x, at the low end of the range above. In this article's reading, the parts of a system bought from third parties (HBM, optics, power, buildings) do not carry Nvidia's markup on either side, which dilutes any chip-level gap at rack level. Nvidia also has a lever. Its Q4 guidance steps down to 71–72%, and on the 17 November call interim CEO Colette Kress called Q4 "our bottom"4142. Kress was briefly in charge: Huang resigned on 12 November40 and the board reinstated him as CEO on 23 November46. That is why SemiAnalysis's post called him "NVIDIA's former and current CEO"45.

Why this matters. If the edge is mostly margin, it is a pricing decision, not a physics result. Nvidia can narrow it by giving up margin, and Google and Amazon can widen it the same way. Either side can move it within a quarter, much faster than a tape-out.

08Revealed preference: the buyers who could check

The strongest corroboration comes from buyers who can run their own benchmarks and have signed for these chips at enormous scale. The weakest point in that corroboration is that every one of them also kept buying Nvidia, and most of the deals came with investment from the chip vendor.

Anthropic agreed to use up to one million TPUs, with "well over a gigawatt" online in 2026, and described "a diversified approach that efficiently uses three chip platforms"23. In April 2026 it added "multiple gigawatts of next-generation TPU capacity" through Google and Broadcom36. It also committed to up to 5 GW of new AWS capacity spanning Trainium generations, and more than $100 billion of AWS spending over ten years24. OpenAI committed to "approximately 2 gigawatts of Trainium capacity through AWS"26. That came months after a $38 billion AWS deal for Nvidia GB200 and GB300 capacity27. Anthropic also signed $30 billion with Azure for up to 1 GW of Nvidia Grace Blackwell and Vera Rubin systems, alongside up to $10 billion of Nvidia investment25. Jassy said on the Q3 call that "short-term capacity for prior generation Trainium instances sell at a premium compared to the first half of the year"22. On Alphabet's Q3 call, Google Cloud said nearly 100 customers use TPUs and guided to "a meaningful revenue ramp in 2027" for TPU system sales35.

The counter-evidence is about who can capture the edge. A leaked July 2025 Amazon document reported by Business Insider said Cohere found Trainium less competitive than H100 and Stability AI found Trainium2 less competitive on speed and cost. Amazon described Trainium2 users as mainly "a small number of large customers, such as Anthropic"30.

Counterpoint. Prior-generation Trainium selling at a premium is a sign of scarce compute, not proof of a cost edge; in a supply-constrained market, any accelerator sells. And investment-linked purchases are weak revealed preference, since Amazon funds the buyer that buys its chips2426.

Why this matters. The buyers best placed to check SemiAnalysis's claim are behaving as if a cost edge exists for them. They are also hedging as if it might not last.

09What could break the thesis

This article's thesis is that the TPU and Trainium edge is real but mostly a pricing result, concentrated among large-volume buyers and against Blackwell. Four findings would overturn it, in either direction.
