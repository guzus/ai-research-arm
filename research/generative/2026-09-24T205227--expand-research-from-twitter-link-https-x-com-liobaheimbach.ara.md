## 01. The promise: what protected order flow claims to do

For most of Ethereum's history, every pending transaction sat in a public waiting room before a block producer picked it up. Anyone running a node could see a trade before it settled — its sender, its target contract, the exact swap it intended — and act on that knowledge first. In August 2020, Paradigm's Dan Robinson and Georgios Konstantopoulos gave that waiting room a name that stuck: not a marketplace, but "something worse: a dark forest," where predators lurk silently for exploitable transactions to wander past [^3]. The metaphor caught on because it was accurate. The public mempool was not merely transparent; it was actively hunted.

The academic diagnosis predates the metaphor by a year. Daian et al.'s "Flash Boys 2.0" coined the term miner extractable value (MEV) and documented bots running priority gas auctions — bidding up transaction fees against each other in real time to win the right to front-run a victim's trade [^4]. Zhou et al. later quantified what that hunting actually paid: a single sandwich attacker clearing "over several thousand USD" per day on Uniswap alone, years before any mainstream protection existed [^6]. Qin, Zhou and Gervais then scaled the picture to the whole chain, tallying **$540.54 million** in extractable value broadly — sandwiches pooled together with arbitrage and liquidations, not sandwiches alone — across 32 months and 11,289 addresses [^5]. The dark forest, in other words, was not a rhetorical flourish. It had a receipt.

The industry's answer to the dark forest was to stop lighting the forest at all. Flashbots Protect, launched in October 2021, routed transactions around the public mempool entirely and into a private relay — the first mainstream "hiding" service, and still the reference design the rest of this space measures itself against [^8]:

:::quote(attr="Flashbots Protect documentation")
Transactions are sent to a private Flashbots mempool where they will be hidden from frontrunning and sandwich bots.
:::

That is a submission-layer guarantee, and only that: it says nothing about what happens to a transaction after it leaves the private mempool and reaches whoever assembles the block. Later mechanisms made this explicit rather than incidental. MEV-Share, which went into beta in April 2023, is architecturally the opposite of pure hiding — it deliberately discloses selective "hints" (a contract address, a function selector, calldata, logs, a transaction hash) to searchers in exchange for MEV rebates paid back to the user [^9]. Flashbots' own documentation frames the resulting trade-off without euphemism: "Sharing more hints can increase refunds, while sharing fewer hints can increase privacy," and maximizing privacy "could make it more challenging for searchers to spot MEV opportunities, leading to a very likely decrease in your MEV refund" [^10]. That is the protocol's own operator conceding that the rebate mechanism and the privacy guarantee pull in opposite directions — though notably its docs never connect that tension explicitly back to sandwich-attack risk. Order-flow auctions and Solana's Jito bundle marketplace generalize the same shape: priority or payment in exchange for information moving somewhere it wasn't before. Not every vendor hedges the claim as carefully as Flashbots does — MEVBlocker markets itself as fully preventing "frontrunning and sandwich attacks" by routing to "a private mempool of trusted searchers," a stronger, less qualified promise than "hidden from" [^11].

That gap — between "hidden from bots" as marketed and "some information still moves, to someone, for a price" as engineered — is precisely what a new 2026 paper set out to measure rather than assume. Heimbach, Solmaz, Öz and Torres' "No Place to Hide: An Analysis on Protected Order Flow Sandwich Attacks" (submitted 23 September 2026) is the first measurement to span three years and six chains — Ethereum, Solana, Tron, Base, Arbitrum and Monad, from July 2023 to June 2026 — asking whether the protections built since the dark-forest era actually close the gap they were sold to close [^1]. Lead author Lioba Heimbach announced it on X the day it posted, framing the whole inquiry as a direct callback to the original metaphor: "Bots watch every pending trade in Ethereum's public mempool and sandwich the ones worth attacking. Hence the nickname: the dark forest" [^44]. What the paper actually found, chain by chain, is the subject of the rest of this article.

One caveat belongs here, before any number gets carried forward: the pre-protection dollar figures cited above — Zhou et al.'s per-day sandwich earnings and Qin et al.'s $540.54M extractable-value tally — describe a different, non-adjacent era of the chain's history (roughly 2015 through the early 2020s) and measure extractable value broadly, not sandwich attacks in isolation. They are not a baseline this article can net against the new paper's 2023–2026 findings to produce a clean "before vs. after" percentage decline; the literature does not support that comparison, and nothing later in this piece should imply otherwise. What both eras of research agree on is simpler and more durable: users are told these mechanisms "protect" their trades, and the rest of this article is about whether that word is doing any real work.

## 02. Inside the study: three years, six chains, one persistent finding

The evidence base for everything that follows in this article is a single measurement study: "No Place to Hide: An Analysis on Protected Order Flow Sandwich Attacks" (Heimbach, Solmaz, Öz, Torres; arXiv:2609.28115, submitted 23 September 2026)[^1]. It bills itself as the first longitudinal, three-year measurement of sandwich attacks specifically against *protected* order flow — the private mempools, auctions, and order-flow-auction (OFA) systems that exist precisely to stop this class of attack — and it runs the analysis across six blockchains: Ethereum, Solana, Tron, Base, Arbitrum, and Monad, over a fixed window of 1 July 2023 through 30 June 2026[^2]. Three years is long enough to separate a transient exploit from a structural one, and six chains is enough to ask whether the failure is chain-specific or systemic. The headline answer, detailed below, turns out to be both: systemic in the sense that it recurs everywhere protection is deployed, and overwhelmingly concentrated in the sense that one chain accounts for essentially all of the dollar damage.

That donut is worth staring at before reading anything else in this article. "Six chains" describes the study's scope, not the distribution of harm. Solana alone contributes 28,042,725 of the roughly 28.1 million detected sandwiches and $345.2M of the $346M+ net total — north of 99.7% of both counts[^2]. Tron ($642,542 net)[^2], Ethereum ($254,566 net)[^2], and Base ($2,416 net)[^2] are real findings, not noise, but together they are a rounding error next to Solana. Arbitrum and Monad produced zero persistent attackers under the paper's methodology[^2]. Any framing of this as an evenly-distributed six-chain phenomenon is wrong; it is a Solana crisis with three small, mechanically distinct footnotes, which is exactly how the rest of this article is organized.

Getting from raw transaction data to those numbers required a detection pipeline built to resist false positives, because sandwich-shaped transaction triples occur by coincidence far more often than by attack. The paper's methodology runs three filters in sequence[^2]. **H1 ("Structure")** looks for the basic shape of a sandwich — a front-running trade and a back-running trade bracketing a victim's trade — while tolerating realistic execution lag: up to five blocks apart on EVM chains, or two leader rotations on Solana, since a real attacker rarely lands both legs in the immediately adjacent slot. **H2 ("One Role")** then prevents a single transaction from being counted as more than one role in more than one sandwich, which would otherwise inflate both the sandwich count and the profit total[^2]. **H3 ("Persistence")** is the filter that matters most for credibility: a wallet only counts as a genuine attacker if it has executed at least 100 sandwiches, is profitable on at least 60% of them, and has sandwich legs making up at least 25% of its total swap activity[^2]. That threshold is deliberately built to exclude a trader who got lucky once or twice and keep only entities that are running sandwiching as a repeatable, profitable strategy — the difference between "this happened" and "this is a business."

The most diagnostically important finding in this section isn't the dollar total — it's a timing pattern that shouldn't exist if protection were merely leaky in some ordinary, incidental way. Ordinary public-mempool sandwiches are tight: the front-running transaction is typically the very next transaction after the victim's, because the attacker is reacting to a visible pending transaction in real time. Protected-order-flow sandwiches on Ethereum look nothing like that. The paper finds a median of 58 transactions between the front leg and the first victim transaction, and 131 transactions between the victim and the back leg[^2]. An attacker who is genuinely blind to the victim's trade until it's mined has no mechanism to insert a front-run 58 transactions in advance — that gap is a signature of information arriving through a slower, out-of-band channel: an OFA hint leaking intent before execution, a private RPC feed being read by someone who shouldn't have access, or a reorg exposing a transaction that was supposed to stay private. In other words, the spacing pattern isn't just correlated with the leakage thesis — it is close to direct evidence for it, because it rules out the ordinary mempool-visibility explanation that would otherwise be the null hypothesis. The same section notes that outside Solana, this activity is run by a strikingly small number of entities — 7 bots on Ethereum, 12 on Tron (4 distinct groups), 4 on Base (3 distinct groups) — versus Solana's 8,631, a concentration gap the article returns to later when asking who these attackers actually are[^2].

One honest caveat belongs here before the per-chain detail: the H3 persistence filter is a floor, not a ceiling. By construction, it throws out any attacker who hasn't yet cleared 100 sandwiches, 60% win rate, and 25% activity share — which means every opportunistic, low-volume, or newly-started sandwicher in the dataset is invisible to these totals by design. A looser methodology tuned to catch more marginal attackers would almost certainly find more sandwiches and more profit, not less, so $346M+ should be read as a conservative lower bound on what protected order flow is actually leaking. That a "protection" layer built to eliminate exactly this attack instead shows a persistent, structurally distinct, multi-year pattern of it — at a scale even the most conservative counting method can't shrink below nine figures — is the finding the rest of this article has to explain chain by chain.

## 03. Solana: the epicenter, and why fixes keep sliding sideways

Solana's sandwich problem is not a rounding error next to the other five chains in this study — it *is* the study, accounting for roughly 99.7% of everything measured. That concentration traces back to architecture, not carelessness. Ethereum-style chains route pending transactions through a public mempool that outside searchers can watch; Solana was designed around leader rotation instead, where a single validator holds the pen for each slot and — critically — could see the transactions queued for that slot before anyone else, including the trader who submitted them. The attacker in a Solana sandwich is not always an external bot racing to see a transaction first. For a meaningful share of the pattern, it can be the entity best positioned of all: the validator itself, or a searcher renting privileged access to that validator's queue.

The scale that architecture produced: 28,042,725 sandwiches, executed by 8,631 distinct entities, for $383.4M gross and $345.2M net after roughly 10% bled off to fees and validator/relay tips[^2].

The ecosystem has not ignored this. Three separate interventions have landed in eighteen months, each targeting a specific point where pending trades were exposed.

:::timeline

- {date: "2024-06", headline: "SFDP delists operators", body: "Solana Foundation removes validator operators running sandwich-enabling mempools from its Delegation Program."}
- {date: "2025-02", headline: "Marinade MIP.9 passes", body: "73 validators (~1.27M SOL) blocklisted from Marinade's Stake Auction Marketplace for repeated sandwiching."}

Start with the mempool itself. Before March 2024, transactions submitted through Jito's Block Engine sat in a relayer for roughly a 200ms auction window, during which searchers could bid to have their bundle — sandwiching trade included — forwarded to the validator ahead of the victim[^14]. That window was the mechanical heart of validator-level exposure, and Jito shut it down outright: "in the absence of an engineering solution we have made the difficult decision to suspend the mempool," said Jito Labs' Lucas Bruder, an explicit admission that the pending-transaction visibility it offered was enabling the attacks it was meant to route around[^13]. Enforcement against validators followed at the operator level: the Solana Foundation Delegation Program removed operators found running sandwich-enabling mempools[^15], with SFDP's Tim Garcia stating flatly, "Decisions in this matter are final. Enforcement actions are ongoing as we detect operators participating in mempools which allow sandwich attacks"[^15]. Marinade DAO went further through governance, passing MIP.9 in February 2025[^16] to blocklist 73 validators — about 1.27M SOL, roughly 15.3% of relevant stake — from its Stake Auction Marketplace over repeated sandwiching[^16].

There is also a transaction-level flag, `jitodontfront`, that any trader can set to opt a transaction out of front-running within Jito's system. Jito's own documentation is unusually candid about its limits: a bundle containing a flagged transaction "will be rejected by the block engine" unless that transaction is placed first — but this "applies only to the block engine and not to any other software," and, in Jito's own words:

:::quote(attr="Jito documentation")
This feature may help reduce sandwich attacks but is not guaranteed to do so.
:::

That disclaimer turns out to describe the flag's real-world performance almost exactly. The paper found that Axiom, a major Solana trading front-end, sets `jitodontfront` on 98.5% of the victim transactions in its dataset — near-universal adoption of the one opt-in protection the ecosystem offers[^2]. And yet only 16.5% of the sandwiches executed against those flagged transactions carried a Jito tip at all[^2] — meaning the large majority of attacking bots were never routing through the block engine the flag governs in the first place. A flag that is close to universally set is, at the same time, close to totally beside the point for most attackers: they simply operate outside the one software surface where the flag has teeth.

The trading front-ends sit downstream of all this, and they are not uniformly exposed. Several — Axiom, Photon, GMGN, BullX, Trojan, Jupiter Ultra — show victim rates well above what their share of trading volume would predict[^2], with the most over-represented app reaching an excess ratio of up to 20.9x[^2]. Controlling for volume this way is more rigorous than a raw victim count, though the paper does not resolve exact per-app numbers beyond that maximum, so the ranking should be read directionally, not as a precise league table[^2]. Axiom's own product documentation is candid in the same register as Jito's: its "Off" tier offers "no protection against MEV bots"[^17], a higher "Secure" tier "will wait for a whitelisted validator to be available"[^17], and the tips used to buy that protection are, in Axiom's words, "NOT 100% effective"[^17]. Two products in this stack, independently, describe their own MEV defenses as best-effort rather than guarantees — which is a rare instance of the industry's own literature matching its measured performance.

None of this means the interventions were pointless. Elevated-leader exposure — attacks concentrated around a specific leader's own slot, the scenario the mempool shutdown and validator delistings targeted most directly — really did fall, down to about 2% of attacks today[^2]. That is a fix working exactly as narrowly designed. But total attack volume didn't follow it down: roughly 20,000 sandwiches still happen per day, with 96% now executed cross-block instead[^2] — meaning the attack simply relocated to an exposure point the interventions weren't built to close. "These fixes accomplished nothing" would overstate the failure; "protection relocates rather than eliminates" is the more honest reading, and a partial win at one layer of a stack this deep is still worth having. It matters because Solana's retail trading volume is disproportionately routed through exactly the front-ends shown here to be most exposed, so the gap between an opt-in flag's adoption and its effectiveness is not academic — it is the gap millions of individual trades are currently falling into.

## 04. Ethereum: the auctions and the accidents leaking private trades

Ethereum's version of this story is smaller by any dollar measure and far less messy to explain. The chain's protected-order-flow ecosystem — order-flow auctions (OFAs) like MEV-Share and MEVBlocker, plus whitelisted-searcher products like Blink — was built specifically to keep pending trades out of the public mempool, and largely does. But the paper finds two distinct, nameable leaks rather than a single systemic hole: hint data that OFAs disclose by design, and stale transactions that reorgs expose by accident. That precision is the payoff of a much smaller headline number. Across the same three-year window, Ethereum sandwich activity totals 30,607 attacks executed by just 7 bots, netting $254,566 after fees — three orders of magnitude fewer attacks than Solana, and roughly six orders of magnitude less money.[^2]

Fees eat the attacks alive — 59.1% of gross revenue goes to the network rather than the attacker[^2], a tax Solana's attackers never pay at anywhere near that rate[^2] — and the population of attackers is tiny to begin with. That combination of low volume and heavy friction is the headline framing; the more interesting question is where the exposure that does exist comes from, and the paper traces most of it to two sources.

The first source is the OFA hint stream itself. 66.8% of attributed victims (of successful attacks specifically) are traceable to appearing in at least one OFA hint dataset — though the authors are careful to say this is co-occurrence, not proof: they cannot establish that a given hint was *the* leak vector for any single victim, only that victims disproportionately surface in these feeds. What sharpens the finding is a technique the paper calls "double-hashing," which lets an attacker correlate the same underlying transaction across two OFA products that were designed as separate, siloed hint streams — MEV-Share (Flashbots) and MEVBlocker (CoW Protocol/Punk Domain/SMG) — by hash-matching disclosed fields. Across the full three-year period, 28.3% of attributed victims turn up in both datasets jointly; in the earlier, pre-Blink sub-period specifically (through February 2025), that joint-overlap rate ran far higher, at 70.4% — a reminder that this exposure has moved around across sub-periods as much as the attacks themselves have.

:::bars
- {label: "Appear in ≥1 OFA dataset (successful attacks)", value: "66.8%", pct: 67}
- {label: "Appear in both MEV-Share + MEVBlocker (full period)", value: "28.3%", pct: 28}
- {label: "Appear in Blink (whitelisted-searcher) dataset", value: "62.2%", pct: 62}
:::

These three figures are victim-exposure co-occurrence rates drawn from overlapping, non-mutually-exclusive datasets over the full study period — the same victim can appear in more than one — so they describe three separate lenses on the same population, not three slices that sum to 100%. The pre-Blink sub-period's 70.4% joint-overlap rate is a real, paper-stated figure too, but it describes a narrower window, not the three-year average, and should not be read as the general case.

The double-hashing result matters because it collapses a design assumption the OFA model depends on: that hint feeds are useful precisely because they're partial. MEV-Share discloses contract address, function selector, calldata, logs, and transaction hash — never the full transaction — and MEVBlocker was built as an independent, competing feed with its own disclosure policy.[^9] Neither alone is supposed to be enough to reconstruct a sandwichable trade. Flashbots' own documentation names the tradeoff explicitly: sharing more hints increases refunds, sharing fewer increases privacy.[^10] Double-hashing shows that once two "fewer hints" feeds exist side by side, an attacker can hash-match the shared fields across both and recover more than either feed intended to reveal — the privacy budget of two products isn't additive, it's compromised jointly, even if the joint-overlap rate has varied across the study's sub-periods.

The second OFA-adjacent leak is structural rather than technical: full-disclosure whitelisting. Blink grants onboarded searchers complete transaction data once approved, which — according to the paper — makes vetting the searcher, not withholding the data, the actual protection; Merkle, an earlier whitelisted-searcher product, reportedly no longer operates independently, with the paper stating it merged with Blink in 2026 (a claim that traces to the paper itself, not an independent Blink or Merkle announcement, and should be read with that caveat). 62.2% of attributed victims appear in the Blink dataset, consistent with a model where trust in who gets whitelisted substitutes for cryptographic non-disclosure — a materially weaker guarantee than the "no one sees the trade" pitch protected order flow is sold on.

The second leak source is not a designed tradeoff at all — it's an accident of consensus. When Ethereum reorganizes, transactions broadcast for inclusion in the discarded block become briefly public again while the chain settles on a successor. The paper documents 9,297 reorged blocks exposing 56,730 previously-private swap transactions this way, of which 2,875 were subsequently sandwiched once they resurfaced in the replacement block. Because the authors recovered only 65.9% of stale, reorged block bodies, that 2,875 figure is very likely an undercount rather than an overcount — meaning the true reorg-driven toll is probably higher than what's measured here.[^2]

The counterpoint worth sitting with: unlike Solana's sprawling 8,631-entity attacker population[^2], Ethereum's problem is narrow. Bytecode and deployment analysis indicates all 7 sandwiching bots are very likely operated by a single entity[^2], with just two of those bots responsible for 78.0% of every attack[^2]. A harm this concentrated is, in principle, addressable by identifying and cutting off one actor — a fundamentally different remediation problem than a free-for-all with thousands of independent operators, even before weighing the dollar amounts involved.

This matters because it separates two failure modes that get conflated in casual MEV discussion: leaked information (an engineering and disclosure-policy problem, fixable by tightening OFA design) versus adversarial scale (a market-structure problem, which is what actually distinguishes Ethereum's outcome from Solana's).

## 05. Tron and Base: the low-tech leaks

Solana's auction-within-an-auction and Ethereum's private-order-flow accidents are, whatever else they are, sophisticated. Both chains built real infrastructure — validator scheduling logic, MEV-aware relays, privacy-preserving mempools — and still leaked. Tron and Base leak for much cruder reasons. Tron's protection is a documentation convention about queue order that anyone can read around; Base's was, for five days in 2023, an RPC endpoint nobody had gotten around to turning off, plus at least one victim who handed attackers a repeating schedule for free. The dollar amounts are correspondingly smaller — three to five orders of magnitude below Solana and Ethereum — but the mechanism is the same failure in miniature: a chain markets an ordering property, and the property does not survive contact with anyone motivated to test it.

:::compare
- {role: "CLAIMED (Justin Sun, X)", name: "Tron front-running prevention", value: "98%"}
- {role: "OBSERVED (weakest bot)", name: "Per-bot sandwich success rate", value: "74%"}
- {role: "OBSERVED (strongest bot)", name: "Per-bot sandwich success rate", value: "96%", subject: true}
:::

Tron's official position, per its own Developer Hub, is unglamorous: block producers pull transactions out of the mempool in first-in-first-out order — "the order it received them" — with no mention anywhere in that documentation of front-running or MEV.[^19] It reads as a consensus bookkeeping detail, not a security guarantee, and the paper is careful to note why that distinction matters: the FCFS rule "is implemented at the client level and could, in principle, be replaced" — it is a software convention every node operator happens to follow, not something Tron's protocol enforces cryptographically.[^2] The 98%-prevention figure that circulates around Tron did not come from that documentation at all; it traces to an informal statement by founder Justin Sun, and the paper quotes it directly before rebutting it: the twelve identified bots land 38,567 sandwiches with per-entity success rates between 74% and 96%.[^2] Those are the individual bots' own hit rates on attempts they chose to make, not a rival prevention-rate computed the same way as Sun's 98% — the honest claim is that a mempool landing that many attacks at that success rate is inconsistent with 98% of front-running being blocked, not that the paper recomputed the missing statistic.

What actually lets bots win Tron's FCFS "race" is `gettransactionlistfrompending`, an officially documented, publicly accessible, no-authentication API that returns every transaction ID currently sitting in Mainnet's pending pool.[^20] Nothing about this is a bug: it is an intentional feature that happens to convert "first come, first served" into "whoever polls fastest and submits closest to the block producer wins." That is what makes Tron's story a latency race dressed up as fairness rather than an accident, and the attack is concentrated enough to make the point starkly — two bots account for 19,247 and 10,438 sandwiches, 77.0% of the total, and nine of the twelve "bots" trace to one shared funding wallet, meaning the ecosystem's supposed multi-bot competition is mostly a single operator polling one public endpoint against itself.[^2]

:::timeline
- {date: "2023-08-22", headline: "op-geth PR #118 merged", body: "Disables pending-transaction filters and WebSocket subscriptions on Base RPC nodes."}
- {date: "2023-08-23", headline: "op-geth PR #122 merged", body: "Disables the local transaction pool entirely for sequencer-fronting RPC nodes."}
- {date: "2023-09-01", headline: "PR #118 reverted", body: "The pending-subscription filter fix is walked back ten days later — the story doesn't end cleanly."}
:::

Base's leak in the paper's dataset is smaller still — 1,889 sandwiches from four bots in three time-separated groups, $3,031 gross and $2,416 net — but it is a genuinely different species of failure: a literal transaction-pool exposure in op-geth.[^2] The August 2023 response disabled the pending-transaction filters and WebSocket subscriptions that let outside observers watch the mempool (PR #118), then went further and killed the local transaction pool outright for RPC nodes running with `--rollup.sequencerhttp` set (PR #122).[^21][^22] That would be a clean before/after story except that PR #118 itself was reverted ten days later, on September 1 — the pending-subscription filter came back, and the record does not show this as a leak that got patched and stayed patched.[^21]

Not every dollar of Base's exposure needed a leak at all. One victim wallet — `0xd3fcD6bFD7Ab2613Cc0275644b74Dcbe7BEb0F17`[^2] — was sandwiched repeatedly for a much simpler reason: it sold roughly the same amount of WETH for a different token about every two minutes[^2], in a near-identical scripted cycle it repeated more than 230 times.[^2] An attacker watching that wallet needed no privileged mempool access, no API polling, nothing the chain could patch — just enough patience to notice the pattern and set a timer.

A separate, later Base incident — outside the paper's sandwich dataset, but a useful second data point on the same chain — shows the same lesson through a different mechanism. Base's "Flashblocks" feature, marketed for faster and fairer execution, was exploited by two snipers who extracted more than $1.3M during a same-block token launch tied to Base founder Jesse Pollak, by watching 200ms micro-block visibility and outbidding rivals through sequencer priority-fee tips — one trader reportedly paid the sequencer over $44,000 to win.[^23] That is launch-sniping via fee auction, not an RPC leak or a predictable victim, and it shouldn't be read as the same bug recurring; it is a reminder that "protected" and "faster" are marketing claims a chain makes about itself, and each one needs its own adversarial test.

None of this moves the needle next to Solana's hundreds of millions. But Tron and Base earn their place in this study precisely because they are boring failures: an ordering rule that assumes nobody will poll a public endpoint faster than the block producer, and a software default nobody had gotten around to hardening. Sophisticated auction design can leak. So can a mempool nobody remembered to lock down — which matters most for any chain currently assuming its own simplicity is a substitute for a security guarantee.

## 06. The two clean chains: architecture, or just too young to attack?

Two chains in this study came back with zero persistent sandwich attackers under the same H1/H2/H3 detection methodology that found rampant, sophisticated extraction on Solana, Ethereum, Tron and Base: Arbitrum and Monad.[^2] That symmetry is where the resemblance ends. Arbitrum shares its transaction-processing shape with Base — both run through a centralized sequencer — yet came back clean where Base did not. Monad shares its throughput ambitions with Solana — both lean on parallelism to push volume — yet came back clean where Solana is the epicenter of the entire problem.[^2] Two chains, one result, and two explanations that deserve to be told apart: Arbitrum's zero rests on a documented, independently-corroborated design choice; Monad's zero rests mostly on the calendar.

:::kv
- {term: "Arbitrum ordering rule", def: "Strict FCFS by arrival time (Offchain Labs docs)"}
- {term: "Arbitrum's trust assumption", def: "Sequencer is honest and doesn't front-run its own users"}
- {term: "Monad mainnet launch", def: "24 Nov 2025 — under 1 year of data in this study"}
- {term: "Monad's mempool design", def: "No shared global mempool; local per-leader queues"}
:::

Arbitrum's case is the stronger one, and it is stronger for a specific, checkable reason. Offchain Labs' own documentation states the sequencer orders transactions by strict first-come-first-served arrival time, not by gas bid: "Under first-come, first-serve (FCFS), arrival time sets the order."[^24] That is not an incidental property — Offchain Labs has described this baseline FCFS policy in its own words as protecting users "from harmful MEV like front-running & sandwich attacks," with Timeboost layered on top later as an additional mechanism rather than a replacement for the underlying default.[^25] Crucially, this paper is not the only one to have looked. A separate, earlier 2024 academic measurement study, working over a different time window, independently reported it "did not detect any sandwiching activity on popular rollups," attributing the result to the absence of a public pending-transaction mempool — only the sequencer sees transactions before finality.[^26] Two independent studies, two different windows, the same finding on Arbitrum specifically: that convergence is real evidence against the theory that "zero" is just an artifact of a strict detection threshold. But the honesty has to run both directions. That same 2024 paper is explicit that this is a trust guarantee, not a cryptographic one — the sequencer is assumed honest, and a centralized sequencer *could* sandwich its own users if it chose to.[^26] Absence of detected attacks is not proof of an inability to attack; it is proof that, so far, the trusted party hasn't been caught defecting.

Monad's case looks superficially similar but rests on much thinner ground. Its architecture avoids a single shared global mempool: transactions are forwarded directly to the next scheduled leaders' own local mempools, according to independent technical analysis of the protocol — notably not a claim the Monad Foundation itself has made as an anti-sandwich design rationale, since no such official statement exists.[^27] That structural difference from Solana's broadcast mempool is real, but the paper's own explanation for Monad's clean result is not "the architecture prevented it" — it is a plain statistical-power caveat. Monad's mainnet has been live since 24 Nov 2025, giving this study under a year of history to work with, against Solana's operating history back to March 2020.[^2] The authors themselves treat a clean Monad as likely too early to call rather than a validated safety result. Worth flagging here, and returned to later, is that Monad's parent company, Category Labs, employs this paper's lead author — a detail this article returns to in section 08.[^28] The asymmetry is the point: Arbitrum's zero has two independent studies and a documented mechanism behind it; Monad's zero has neither yet, and one clean stretch of months does not carry the same evidentiary weight as Arbitrum's multi-year, multi-study absence.

:::callout(kind=warn, label="Detector limits")
"Zero persistent attackers" describes what a detector requiring at least 100 sandwiches and a 60% win rate can see. A slow-building or low-volume attacker on either chain — patient enough to stay under that persistence bar — would be invisible to this methodology for some time yet. Read "zero" as "none found meeting a high bar," not "provably safe."
:::

Anyone evaluating a new chain's MEV-safety pitch should ask which "zero" they're actually being sold — one grounded in a documented mechanism and replicated across independent studies, or one that is simply too new to have been tested — because both currently produce the identical headline number.

## 07. Sizing the harm: is $346M big or small?

Every number in this report so far has been a numerator without a denominator. $345.2M extracted from Solana traders and $254,566 from Ethereum traders over three years are large-sounding figures in isolation, but "large" is meaningless without a base to divide by. Put against the trillions of dollars that actually moved through these chains' decentralized exchanges in the same period, does protected-order-flow leakage register as a crisis, or as a rounding error too small to justify the engineering effort this report has spent describing it?

:::bars
- {label: "Ethereum (of ~$674B 2024 DEX volume)", value: "~0.004 bps", pct: 1}
- {label: "Solana (of ~$2.4-2.5T 2024-25 DEX volume)", value: "~1.3 bps", pct: 100}
:::

The bar heights above are illustrative of relative scale, not a precise linear percentage — 0.004 bps and 1.3 bps span more than two orders of magnitude, and compressing that range into a 1-to-100 bar chart necessarily flattens it. Treat the chart as "which chain leaks more, and by roughly how many orders of magnitude," not as a proportional plot.

The arithmetic behind those numbers is this article's own back-of-envelope synthesis, not a figure the underlying paper states. Taking Solana's $345.2M net sandwich profit[^2] against a secondary-aggregator estimate of roughly $2.4-2.5 trillion in 2024-2025 Solana DEX volume yields approximately 1.2-1.4 basis points of volume extracted. Doing the same for Ethereum — $254,566[^2] against roughly $674B in 2024 DEX volume — yields approximately 0.004 basis points. That gap implies Solana's leakage rate runs somewhere on the order of 25-370x higher than Ethereum's, even though Ethereum's raw sandwich count over the window, 30,607 attacks[^2], is not itself a small number[^7]. Every piece of that ratio is approximate: the denominators come from secondary sources covering 2024-2025 only, not the paper's exact three-year window, and DEX volume estimates vary meaningfully between aggregators. Treat the ratio as directional, not audited.

Taken at face value, the steelman case is strong. A market running at roughly 1.3 basis points of leakage on trillions of dollars of adversarial, permissionless, MEV-searcher-infested volume is arguably performing about as well as anyone should expect infrastructure with no gatekeeper, no KYC, and no enforcement body to perform — single-digit-basis-point costs are the kind of number market participants in most trading venues absorb as routine friction rather than treat as headline news. By that yardstick, someone could reasonably argue that protected order flow is doing its job, that Jito's anti-sandwich flag and MEVBlocker-style routing are working at scale, and that this entire report is chasing a decimal point.

That case collapses the moment the average is decomposed. Axiom sets the `jitodontfront` anti-sandwich flag on 98.5% of the 21.5 million Solana transactions this study found sandwiched[^2] — near-universal adoption of the one protective mechanism retail users actually have. Those are not bystanders who never opted in; they are the users who did exactly what they were told to do, and got sandwiched anyway, at the scale documented in section 03. A basis-point average describes the whole market's aggregate cost. It says nothing about how that cost is distributed within the market — and here it is concentrated almost entirely on the population that tried hardest to avoid it. An average that looks reassuring at the market level can still describe near-total failure for the subset of users the protection was built to serve, and that subset, not the market average, is who protected order flow was marketed to.

:::quote(attr="Gary Gensler, then-SEC Chair, 2021")
A ban [on payment for order flow] is a rule that's on the table... it's an inherent conflict of interest.
:::

American equities already ran this exact argument to its conclusion once. Payment for order flow — brokers routing retail orders to wholesale market makers for a per-share rebate — has existed long enough that the SEC first mandated enhanced disclosure over it in 1994, explicitly because of the conflict of interest it creates between the broker's revenue and the customer's execution price[^37]. That framing — conflict of interest, not magnitude — is the same one Gensler invoked in 2021 when he put an outright ban "on the table"[^39]. And regulators did eventually put a number on the harm: in 2020 the SEC found Robinhood had misrepresented PFOF as immaterial to its business while failing its duty of best execution, quantifying $34.1M in aggregate customer harm from worse prices — and then fined Robinhood $65M, nearly double the harm figure[^38]. TradFi's regulator did not treat "the harm per trade is small" as a reason to stop looking; it quantified the harm at exactly that small per-trade scale and penalized it anyway.

It would be dishonest to present this as a solved playbook crypto can adopt. The SEC's more ambitious 2022 proposal — replacing PFOF-style internalization with competitive order auctions, the closest TradFi equivalent to what protected-order-flow advocates want for DEXs — was never finalized. On 2025-06-12 the SEC formally withdrew it along with thirteen other pending proposals, stating it does not intend to issue final rules on any of them[^40]. As of today, PFOF remains legal and essentially unrestricted at the federal rulemaking level. TradFi's own fix for this exact category of problem is itself unresolved and has reverted to the pre-reform status quo. This history is useful for establishing the stakes — that a small-percentage conflict of interest can still be worth a regulator's sustained attention for three decades — not for supplying crypto a template that actually worked.

The deeper gap isn't that one market has a rebate mechanism and the other doesn't — both do. MEVBlocker and Jito's tip system are crypto's OFA equivalents, and they are entirely voluntary and protocol-bonded[^11][^12]. What TradFi has, even in its unfinished, imperfectly-enforced state, is a body with subpoena power and fining authority that can and does investigate a broker's PFOF arrangements and put a dollar figure on the harm. Crypto's OFA mechanisms have no statutory best-execution duty behind them and no equivalent of the SEC or FINRA watching whether the rebate promise is actually kept. That asymmetry, not the basis-point count, is what "is $346M big or small" is actually asking.

## 08. Who studied whom: incentives, disclosure, and what hasn't been peer-reviewed yet

Four names sit on this paper: Lioba Heimbach (Category Labs), Ozan Solmaz (ETH Zurich), Burak Öz (Flashbots), and Christof Ferreira Torres (INESC-ID and Instituto Superior Técnico, University of Lisbon) [^1][^2]. The affiliations are stated plainly in the paper itself, with no accompanying conflict-of-interest disclosure in the accessible text — so working out what those affiliations imply is left to the reader, which is what this section does.

:::kv
- {term: "Lioba Heimbach", def: "Category Labs (builds Monad) — prior work: AsiaCCS 2022, USENIX Security 2025"}
- {term: "Ozan Solmaz", def: "ETH Zurich"}
- {term: "Burak Öz", def: "Flashbots (operates MEV-Share) — prior work: AFT 2024"}
- {term: "Christof Ferreira Torres", def: "INESC-ID / IST Lisbon — no chain or OFA affiliation"}
:::

Two of those affiliations are worth pausing on, because they point in opposite directions rather than the same one. Heimbach works at Category Labs, the company that continues building the Monad protocol after the nonprofit Monad Foundation split off in December 2024 [^28] — and Monad is, alongside Arbitrum, one of the two chains this paper finds "clean" (section 06). That is an incentive to make Monad look good. Öz works at Flashbots, which operates MEV-Share — one of the OFA products this very paper finds leaking at 66.8%/70.4% co-occurrence rates (section 04). That is, if anything, an incentive in the reverse direction: an employee publishing findings that make his own employer's product look bad is the *less* likely outcome of a captured process, not the more likely one. When a paper's plausible biases pull against each other instead of stacking in one direction, that is a more reassuring position than either a single unidirectional conflict or a claim of "no conflicts at all" — aligned incentives, not the mere presence of an employer, are what actually predict slanted results, and here the incentives are not aligned.

Track record outside the current employer matters too, and three of the four authors have one. Heimbach's involvement with MEV research predates Category Labs by years: "Eliminating Sandwich Attacks with the Help of Game Theory" (with Roger Wattenhofer, AsiaCCS 2022)[^42] and a USENIX Security 2025 paper deanonymizing Ethereum validators via P2P network leaks[^42] are both work that exists independent of whatever Monad's interests are today. Öz's Flashbots affiliation sits alongside a genuine measurement record, not an obviously captured marketing role — "Who Wins Ethereum Block Building Auctions and Why?" (AFT 2024) is real academic output on exactly this subject — and Flashbots as an organization has previously published its own transparency reports self-disclosing weaknesses in its systems, including past relay exploits, so an employee publishing findings critical of an OFA product Flashbots operates is not without institutional precedent inside that specific shop. Torres is the cleanest case by a simple standard: prior work in smart-contract-vulnerability research (ASIACCS 2020, USENIX Security 2023) with no evident tie to any chain or OFA product named in this paper — a co-author with no stake in which way sections 03 through 06 come out.

:::callout(kind=warn, label="Not yet peer-reviewed")
This is currently an arXiv-only preprint, submitted 23 September 2026 — two days before this article was researched. No "Comments" field indicating conference or journal acceptance appears on the listing, and no venue-acceptance announcement was found anywhere else. That is a real, current limitation: no formal peer review has yet checked the methodology, the H3 persistence thresholds, or the $346M/28M headline figures. It should temper confidence, not dismiss the findings — several of this paper's own cited predecessors in MEV measurement (Daian et al., Qin/Zhou/Gervais, Zhou et al.) were themselves influential arXiv preprints before or alongside formal publication, so "preprint" in this specific subfield has not historically meant "unreliable."
:::

The strongest available check on any of this is independent replication, and that has not happened yet either. The paper ships a public companion repository [^43] organized into `evm/`, `solana/`, and `export/` directories, with a README describing the same detection pipeline the paper does — per-chain DuckDB storage, the same 100+/60%+/25%+ persistence thresholds used for the H3 filter. That is a genuine transparency artifact, not vaporware: the code that supposedly produced these numbers exists and matches the paper's own description of itself. But as of this research it shows a single commit and zero stars, forks, or watchers — the signature of a fresh submission-time dump, not a repository anyone outside the author team has run, forked, or scrutinized. No one has yet independently confirmed that the code reproduces the $346M and 28M-transaction figures end to end. Separately, no public reaction, rebuttal, or acknowledgment from Flashbots, CoW Protocol/MEVBlocker, or Blink Labs referencing this paper has surfaced — but the paper was only two days old at the time of this research, so that silence reads as "not yet responded to," not as tacit agreement or an absence of rebuttal.

The honest way to hold this paper, then, is: credible authorship, a real and documented methodology, genuinely conflicting incentives that mostly cancel each other out — and not yet independently replicated or peer-reviewed. That combination is a stronger epistemic position than either extreme available to a reader in a hurry: it is neither "trust the number outright" nor "an industry insider wrote this, discount it."

## 09. What would break this, and what would actually fix it

Every section so far has taken the paper's framing at face value: protection leaks, chain by chain, mechanism by mechanism. Before closing, it's worth asking the harder question directly — what would make this article's argument wrong, and does the industry have a real fix waiting in the wings, or only a slogan?

The single weakest link in the causal chain is Ethereum's OFA co-occurrence evidence from section 04. The paper finds 66.8% of attributed victims appear in at least one order-flow-auction hint dataset, and its own authors are careful to say this is correlation, not proof — they "cannot establish" that a given hint was the actual leak vector for any individual victim. That caveat deserves to be taken seriously rather than filed as academic throat-clearing. Flashbots itself has reported that private mempools carry roughly half of all Ethereum gas, and that share has been rising[^41]. If a large and growing fraction of all Ethereum activity — sandwiched or not — already routes through some private channel, then a victim showing up in "at least one OFA dataset" 66.8% of the time is not automatically damning; it could partly reflect how common private routing has become for everyone, not a specific leak unique to the victims who got hit. The paper's Solana findings don't have this problem — they rest on more direct mechanistic evidence, like the mempool timing window and the flag-adoption-versus-tip-correlation mismatch — but the Ethereum causal story leans harder on dataset overlap than this article's earlier sections may have let on, and a critic pressing on exactly that seam would be raising a fair point, not a bad-faith one.

Set against that soft spot is a genuine point of convergence. A separate, independent, methodologically distinct paper — "Sandwiched and Silent," studying a two-month Ethereum window in late 2024 — found the same qualitative result using a different detection heuristic: private and protected transactions still get sandwiched[^36]. Its estimated monthly rate runs roughly 20x higher than the rate implied by this paper's three-year average, a gap this article attributes mostly to a hotter sample period and a looser, non-persistence-filtered detection rule rather than to a real contradiction. Go back further and an even earlier measurement — a 2024 remeasurement of Ethereum-wide sandwich activity across 2015–2023[^7] — counted over three million total sandwich transactions on the chain, all-mempool, not protected-flow-specific[^7]. None of these three studies share a detection method, a time window, or even a research team, and they don't agree on a number. What they agree on is the shape: sandwiching against Ethereum trades that were supposed to be shielded keeps recurring, under every methodology anyone has pointed at the problem. That kind of convergence is worth more than any single paper's headline figure, because it would survive one paper being wrong.

There's also a reason to expect this problem to be permanently unsolvable in full, not merely still-unsolved. Chitra's formal treatment of MEV under uncertainty argues that neither fair-ordering rules nor economic mechanisms, taken individually, can mitigate MEV for arbitrary payoff functions[^32] — a mathematical claim that lines up with what section 03 found empirically on Solana: close one exposure point (the leader-level mempool) and the same underlying incentive relocates to the next one (cross-block patterns) rather than disappearing. Some legal scholars go further and argue the accountability question is itself unresolved: current market-manipulation liability tests weren't built with mechanisms like MEV-Share in mind, and enforcement runs into the same decentralization that makes these systems censorship-resistant in the first place[^45]. If ordering has any economic value at all, something with an incentive to see that order early will keep trying to see it — the question this paper's chain-by-chain results actually answer is not "can leakage be eliminated" but "where does it currently show up," and that list will keep changing as each named leak gets patched.

:::callout(kind=warn, label="What would change this article's mind")
Independent replication of the paper's public GitHub repository reproducing the $346M and 28M-attack figures end to end would raise confidence in every number here. A peer-reviewed venue identifying a flaw in the H1/H2/H3 detection heuristics would lower it. And direct evidence that the 66.8%/28.3%/62.2% Ethereum OFA co-occurrence rates are statistically indistinguishable from ordinary base-rate overlap — given how much Ethereum traffic already routes privately — would meaningfully weaken the Ethereum-specific causal story, though not the Solana findings, which do not depend on dataset co-occurrence in the same way.
:::

The most credible full technical fix — encrypting the mempool so no one, not even a validator, can read a transaction before it's ordered — has been proposed for years and still hasn't shipped at scale. Shutter Network's threshold-encryption design is live on Gnosis Chain and newly extending toward Ethereum's block-building pathway[^29], but the mechanism carries a real cost: raising the number of parties required to jointly decrypt a transaction strengthens resistance to early or malicious decryption, and directly weakens liveness, since losing enough of that committee can stall the chain outright[^31]. Shutter's own team concedes a related point raised by critics — that a colluding subset of the decryption committee could decrypt early and leave no public evidence of having done so — while arguing the design still meaningfully strengthens real-time censorship resistance overall[^30]. Flashbots' own answer to this, SUAVE, was pitched in 2022 with Flashbots stating "we do not intend to participate in the marketplace we build beyond bootstrapping it" — aiming to be "the neutral marketplace designer — not a participant"[^34]; its flagship client repository was quietly archived in May 2025, a strong circumstantial signal that the industry's most ambitious proposed fix has not materialized at the scale its own announcement implied, three-plus years on[^35]. And a more radical critique rejects the entire OFA paradigm as a fix at all: researchers Juels, Eyal and Kelkar have called Flashbots-style solutions "theft," and Ed Felten has described them as "a poorly designed tax on users" — a position that treats rebate-sharing not as harm reduction but as the extraction economy legitimizing itself[^33].

None of this should be read as license to discount the paper's central finding. What's genuinely uncertain here is not whether protected-flow leakage exists — three independent studies, different teams, different methods, converge on yes — but how confidently any single dollar figure or percentage in this article should be attributed to any one named cause, and whether a full fix is a matter of better engineering or a tradeoff no chain has yet been willing to make.

An adversarial pass on this article's three most load-bearing claims — Solana's $345.2M/28.0M-attack headline, the single-entity conclusion behind Ethereum's seven bots, and the rebuttal of Tron's 98% prevention claim — turned up no independent source contradicting any of them; each remains, for now, standing only on this one paper's own analysis, which is a different thing from being independently confirmed. Read that as "unbroken so far," not "proven."
