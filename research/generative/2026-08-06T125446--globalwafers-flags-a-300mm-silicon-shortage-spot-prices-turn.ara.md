The question posed by the headline is whether GlobalWafers has flagged a
genuine 300mm silicon shortage — spot prices turning, long-term agreements
stretching from three years out to eight. The short answer is that two of
those three claims are true in a narrower sense than the framing implies, and
the third is not new at all. What is genuinely new is more interesting than
the headline: the industry has begun financing its own capacity out of its
customers' balance sheets, and the contract structure that makes that possible
is the same structure preventing the shortage from showing up in price.

## 01. What GlobalWafers actually said — and the number that got rounded up

The claim that wafer long-term agreements are stretching "from three years to
eight" is a real quote, lightly mangled. On the Q2 2026 call Doris Hsu,
GlobalWafers' chairperson, described the *previous* cycle: "Last cycle, most of
the LTAs are around three years… The longest will be 5 to 8 years, this time
seems that more, is a bit longer than last time."[^1] Three-to-eight is the
range GlobalWafers is comparing *against*, not the move it is reporting. Hsu
added that the company had only just begun signing this round's contracts and
could answer more precisely in about six months — no median tenor for 2026
LTAs has been disclosed.[^1]

The three-to-eight-year band is also the published industry norm, and has been
for years: TrendForce described the standard silicon-wafer LTA duration as
"three to 8 years," specifying annual supply and demand quantities, in an
article written in August 2023 — from the depths of a *downturn*.[^4] More
damaging to the novelty claim, GlobalWafers itself told the Taipei Times in
December 2021 that its agreements had lengthened from five years in 2007 to
"eight years now," alongside a backlog above NT$100 billion.[^2]

:::callout(kind=warn, label="Premise check")
The eight-year LTA is not a 2026 signal. It is a 2021 signal, made at the top
of a shortage call that was wrong within twenty-four months. In the same
December 2021 interview Hsu said: "We have clear order visibility for 2023 and
it should be okay into 2024… We do not see any signs of things slowing down in
2023 or 2024."[^2] By November 2023 GlobalWafers was reporting small-diameter
utilization of 60–65% and sequentially falling revenue as customers digested
inventory.[^3]
:::

What *is* new is Micron's ten-year term, which Hsu called the longest agreement
in company history — though in the original Chinese her phrasing on value was
conditional: "This is our longest long-term agreement to date, and the total
value should also be the largest."[^43] Reporting that GlobalWafers' prior
record was eight years comes from journalist paraphrase rather than a direct
quote,[^44] which is worth flagging because it is the pivot on which the
"three to eight to ten" narrative turns.

The present-tense tightness claim is genuine and precisely bounded. Management on
the same call: "We
believe the supply-demand tightening in the semiconductor wafer industry is
already underway and is already happening… supply for certain advanced 12-inch
products and applications already become noticeably constrained."[^7] The
qualifier "certain advanced 12-inch products" is doing heavy lifting — this is
scarcity of *qualified capacity for high-specification parts*, not of silicon
discs. Both things can be true at once, and only one of them supports a
generalised shortage thesis.

Why this matters: an eight-year LTA presented as novel evidence of scarcity is
circular reasoning — the same evidence was presented in 2021 and preceded a
25% overcapacity cycle. The tenor of a contract tells you about risk
allocation, not about the balance of supply and demand.

## 02. The price is the test, and the price has not moved

A shortage is not a feeling about utilization. It is a price. And the wafer
industry has an unusual property that makes the price hard to read: most of the
volume is not repriceable in any given year.

The three listed Western and Japanese suppliers said three different things
about pricing within six days of each other, and the differences are the
finding.

Hsu, on 4 August: non-LTA wafers are "definitely higher than H1 this year," and
those prices reset every three to six months. On contract volume: "For LTA
price, usually we honor the price, unless that we have some special terms in
our LTA."[^7] She declined to quantify any percentage increase for either spot
or new contracts, noting that "no matter how strong the market, how strong the
demand is, price negotiation is always very challenging."[^7]

SUMCO was blunter still. Its chief financial officer told the May call the
company was "very determined not to lower prices."[^11] ==unverified: SUMCO's
Q1 filing is widely reported as saying 300mm shipments fell on customer
inventory adjustments while long-term contract prices were held, but I could
not confirm that sentence on the filing page cited.==[^10] Its Q2 results,
released at 15:30 JST on 6 August 2026 — hours before this article's cut-off —
were reported at sales of ¥113.6 billion against ¥112.0 billion guided and an
operating loss of ¥1.1 billion against ¥2.5 billion guided: a beat on volume
and cost, with no evidence of a realised price increase.[^12] Half-year sales
of ¥215.0 billion and a recurring loss of ¥12.2 billion are visible in the wire
report, and the company still declined to issue full-year guidance.[^12]

Siltronic's chief executive Michael Heckmeier gave the sharpest counter-signal
of the three, on 30 July: "What we see today in spot pricing, it's still below
LTA level, and it's clearly below reinvest level." He added that "there seems
still to be a bit, maybe let's call it a bit more wafer supply than
demand."[^9]

That ordering is the entire argument against the shortage narrative in its
strong form. If spot sits below contract and contract sits below the price
that justifies new investment, then the rising spot price GlobalWafers
describes is a recovery from a distressed base, not a scarcity premium. Both
statements can be simultaneously accurate: prices are going up, and they are
still too low.

The income statements corroborate the pessimists. GlobalWafers' Q2 revenue was
NT$15.21 billion, up 8.8% sequentially but down 5.0% year on year, with gross
margin of 20.6% against 25.8% a year earlier.[^40,39] Half-year revenue fell
7.6% and gross margin 5.5 points, which management attributed to pricing agreed
during last year's weak market that has not yet captured the recent demand
recovery, higher qualification and ramp-up costs at new sites, and building
depreciation at the Texas facility.[^7] Siltronic reported Q2 EBITDA of
€69.4 million on sales of €321.6 million — but EBIT of −€51.9 million and a net
loss of €63.2 million, because half-year depreciation nearly doubled to €238.8
million from €126.1 million.[^8]

:::statement(attr="ARA Research")
A company realising an eighteen-to-twenty-two percent price increase does not
report gross margin down five points. The utilization is real; the pricing
power is a forecast.
:::

The one genuine exception is Shin-Etsu, the largest supplier, which grew
consolidated operating profit 4.2% year on year to ¥173.7 billion in the
April–June quarter and told its July briefing that it is receiving supply
requests exceeding production capacity for many items.[^47] Shin-Etsu does not
break out silicon wafers separately — they sit inside a diversified electronic
materials segment — so no wafer margin can be read from its disclosure. But it is the sharpest tightness statement any of the five has
made, and it deserves weight precisely because it comes from the market leader.

Counterpoint worth taking seriously: depreciation, not price, explains most of
the losses. Siltronic's EBITDA margin is 21.6% and holding; the swing to
negative EBIT is a Singapore fab ramping.[^8] SUMCO is running roughly ¥33.6
billion of quarterly depreciation against ¥19.4 billion of half-year capex.[^12]
Strip the fixed-cost wave and the cash businesses are fine. That is fair — but
it cuts both ways, because it means the industry's reported weakness is not
evidence of weak demand, and its reported strength when depreciation rolls off
in 2027–28 will not be evidence of a shortage either.

## 03. Two numbers that do not survive contact with SEMI

Two figures have propagated through coverage of this story. Neither holds.

**The supply gap.** The widely repeated claim is that global 12-inch demand
runs around 11 million wafers a month against effective supply under 10
million, rising to 13 million by 2027. Check the units. SEMI reported worldwide
silicon area shipments of 3,573 million square inches in Q2 2026.[^13] A 300mm
wafer is about 109.4 square inches. Dividing gives roughly **10.9 million
300mm-equivalent wafers per month across every diameter shipped on earth** —
polished and epitaxial, 300mm, 200mm, 150mm and below. The claimed "11 million
of 300mm demand" therefore exceeds the total physical silicon area the industry
ships in all formats. At a realistic 300mm share of area, actual 300mm
shipments run closer to 7–8 million wafers a month. The 2027 figure of 13
million exceeds SEMI's own forecast of installed 300mm fab capacity for
2028.[^49] The gap is a unit error, most likely a comparison of two different
years of the same series.

**The price letters.** The percentages attached to a supposed July 2026 round
of price-increase letters from the three largest suppliers — 5–8% on standard
12-inch logic wafers, 18–22% on AI and HPC grades, 10–25% on HBM-adjacent
high-resistivity substrates — are traceable only to a single Chinese-language
trade aggregation dated 26 June 2026, which attaches none of them to a named
company, filing or research house.[^55] On the same date, Taiwan's Commercial
Times reported that 12-inch pricing was still under negotiation, and that "the
increase customers accept still falls short of the company's
expectations."[^15] No supplier has confirmed a percentage. ==unverified: coverage of SUMCO's Q2 materials places spot pricing at the stage
where renegotiation talks have begun rather than concluded; the underlying deck
is an encrypted PDF that could not be read directly.==[^12]

That divergence is the most important number in this story and almost nobody
quotes it. In 2025 area shipments rose 5.8% to 12,973 million square inches
while wafer revenue *fell* 1.2% to $11.4 billion.[^14] Dividing revenue by
area gives a blended $0.879 per square inch, down from $0.976 in 2023[^51] — a
9.9% decline, and the lowest level in the eight years charted above. Volume recovery with falling revenue is
the arithmetic signature of oversupply, and it is the condition the industry
carried into 2026. The blend mixes diameters and product types, so it is an
index rather than a price; but the direction is unambiguous, and it is
consistent with every 2026 income statement in section 02.

Even the volume trend is decelerating. Year-on-year area growth fell from 13.1%
in Q1 2026 to 7.4% in Q2, and SEMI's own SMG chairman paired the AI-demand
commentary with a warning that "memory price pressures are contributing to
constrained PC and smartphone demand."[^13] Part of that deceleration is a
harder comparison base — sequential growth was still 9.1% — so treat it as
suggestive rather than decisive.

## 04. The demand is real, and it is made of area

Having dismantled the arithmetic, it is important to be equally rigorous about
the other direction: the underlying demand mechanism is genuine, well
documented, and worse than most wafer-demand models assume. AI does not consume
more chips; it consumes more *silicon area per unit of function*, through at
least four independent channels.

The cleanest primary statement comes from Micron's chief executive:
"Industrywide, HBM3E consumes approximately three times the wafer supply as D5
to produce a given number of bits," and "we expect the trade ratio for HBM4 to
be even higher."[^16] That penalty decomposes: SK hynix's D1z DDR4 runs about
0.296 gigabits per square millimetre against roughly 0.16 for HBM3, an area
penalty of about 1.85 times,[^45] with the residual coming from compound stack
yield and known-good-die scrap across a twelve-layer stack.

TrendForce's projection puts the same mechanism in allocation terms: HBM takes
18%, 22% and 30% of total DRAM wafer input at the end of 2025, 2026 and 2027
while supplying 8%, 9% and 13% of DRAM bit supply.[^19] Thirty divided by
thirteen is about 2.3 — HBM consumes roughly 2.3 times its proportionate share
of wafers. That number sits below Micron's three-times figure because it blends
leading-node HBM against all conventional DRAM including older, less dense
product; the two answer different questions and neither contradicts the other.

The second channel is that the classic offset is failing. Node migration has
historically absorbed demand growth by raising bits per square millimetre.
Micron now lists "declining bits per wafer growth from node migrations" as one
of four named constraints on DRAM bit supply, alongside cleanroom constraints,
construction lead times and the HBM trade ratio.[^17] When shrink stops paying,
bits must come from physical wafers.

The third channel is the one wafer models routinely miss: the interposer.
TSMC confirmed in April 2026 that it is "now producing 5.5-reticle size CoWoS"
and planning a 14-reticle version integrating roughly ten large compute dies
and 20 HBM stacks.[^20] A 5.5-reticle interposer is about 4,719 square
millimetres — and it is itself a 300mm wafer start. Run the die-per-wafer
arithmetic — including the wafer-circumference loss term, not a naive area
division — a 4,719 mm² unit yields roughly five gross per wafer, against
roughly 65 gross dies for an 800 mm² compute die.[^20,21] Both figures fall
well below a pure area quotient because large units waste a great deal of the
wafer edge. Per package, the
interposer therefore consumes several times more wafer starts than the
accelerator silicon everyone counts.

That exhibit is a derivation, not a disclosure, and it should be read as one:
NVIDIA has never published an exact die area, stating only that Blackwell uses
"two-reticle limit GPU dies" on TSMC 4NP,[^21] and no manufacturer discloses
HBM core-die area or stack yield. The shares move materially with those
assumptions. The robust conclusion survives anyway: the logic die is the
smallest wafer consumer in its own package, so wafer demand scales with
*packaging* and *memory* decisions far more than with accelerator unit volume.

The fourth channel is mix. GlobalWafers' spokesperson identified 12-inch as the
largest demand opportunity with "silicon photonics and SOI" offering the
fastest growth,[^1] and Hsu named "advanced packaging, HBM, silicon photonics,
and process nodes" as jointly increasing wafer usage.[^1] Neither statement
carried a quantified volume, and worldwide 300mm SOI output is small enough
relative to bulk supply that it is a second-order contributor for now.

What would weaken this section: the offsets are real too. CoWoS-L deliberately
replaces monolithic silicon interposer with a molded RDL carrier and small
embedded bridges — that is genuine wafer thrifting, and it is why Blackwell and
Rubin moved off CoWoS-S. Micron also reports that HBM4 twelve-high is ramping
roughly twice as fast as HBM3E twelve-high and will reach mature yields
"significantly faster,"[^18] which directly attacks the yield half of the
three-times penalty. Wafer demand grows faster than bit demand; it does not
grow as fast as AI capex.

## 05. Why supply cannot answer inside three years

If demand is genuinely area-hungry and prices are genuinely below reinvestment
level, the question becomes how quickly supply can respond. The honest answer
is slower than the standard "two to three years" figure, and the reason is
qualification rather than construction.

GlobalWafers' Sherman, Texas plant is the worked example. Site announced June
2022, groundbreaking December 2022, grand opening May 2025,[^24] phase-one line
complete late 2025, and qualification "completed with multiple customers" only
in Q2 2026.[^22,23] That is roughly 29 months from groundbreaking to a ribbon
cutting and about 42 months to qualified customer supply — and the plant was
still loss-making at that point, on what reporting attributes to "a
slower-than-expected product qualification process, a longer learning curve,
heavy depreciation costs and lower utilization."[^22]

:::callout(kind=info, label="The binding constraint")
Reporting on the Sherman ramp puts qualification of an advanced wafer at
roughly six to nine months, run separately for each customer and device family
and in series with the fab's own device requalification.[^52] The consequence is structural: **a new wafer plant cannot
absorb spot shortage demand.** It can only serve customers who committed years
earlier. This is why prepayments exist — the supplier carries depreciation
before it is allowed to book revenue.
:::

The physics underneath is equally unforgiving. Czochralski crystal growth is a
batch process whose cycle time is set by thermodynamics: defect-controlled
300mm material is pulled at sub-millimetre-per-minute body rates because the
ratio of pull rate to axial thermal gradient determines the vacancy and
interstitial defect regime. Pulling faster to make more wafers degrades exactly
the defect class the leading-edge customer qualified against. Capacity
therefore means more furnaces, more hot zones and more quartz crucibles. Those
crucibles are consumables with a service life measured in the low hundreds of
hours at temperature, and the high-purity quartz they are made from comes
overwhelmingly from two operations in Spruce Pine, North Carolina — a
dependency that scales directly with furnace count.[^56]

Nor is there a diameter escape hatch. The 450mm transition was abandoned when
the G450C consortium unwound in 2017, after the expected per-die cost saving
was revised down from the historical 30% toward roughly 10% as lithography cost
came to dominate and the leading foundry declined to fund it.[^53] There is no wafer-area step function available; every
incremental wafer must come from an incremental furnace, slicing line and epi
reactor.

And the marginal capacity decision in the West is now explicitly a *price*
decision, not a physical one. Siltronic has cleanroom space available inside
its existing Singapore facility and has gated filling it on a 300mm price
recovery that has not happened.[^9] SUMCO went further: in March 2026, into a
supposedly tightening market, it descoped its state-backed expansion, cutting
project cost from ¥225.0 billion to ¥58.0 billion and its maximum METI subsidy
from ¥75.0 billion to ¥19.3 billion, deferring the Yoshinogari greenfield in
favour of upgrading existing Imari lines for leading-edge AI wafers.[^30,31]
Saga Prefecture's land handover slipped a year.[^30]

That is the sharpest single fact in this file. A top-two producer chose *not*
to build into a shortage, because the last overcapacity cycle taught the
oligopoly that margin discipline beats volume. Roughly half the supply lag is
therefore not physics at all — it is deliberate commercial policy, and no
amount of capital shortens it.

Government support does not compress the clock either. GlobalWafers' CHIPS
award is up to $406 million — $380 million for Sherman, $20 million for St.
Peters, Missouri and $6 million in workforce funding — disbursed against
construction, technology, production and commercial milestones.[^25] Its parent
disclosed receipt of just over $200 million in June 2025, roughly half, and no
second tranche has been publicly documented since.[^26] Subsidy arrives *after*
the money is spent; it improves the return, not the lead time.

## 06. The contract is the product: LTAs as risk transfer

Strip away the shortage framing and what the Micron deal actually documents is
a change in who funds semiconductor capacity.

Micron announced up to $3 billion of US supply-chain investment on 9 July 2026,
of which $500 million is "strategic financing support" to advance GlobalWafers
America's Sherman facility, with the two companies to "enter into a 10-year
supply agreement that will provide Micron with access to significant raw
silicon wafer capacity."[^5] Three qualifiers in that primary text are routinely
dropped downstream. The release specifies no structure — no equity, no
convertible, no loan. It uses the future tense. And it states plainly: "The
proposed transaction remains subject to definitive agreements, customary
approvals and closing conditions."[^5] On the 4 August call, GlobalWafers
confirmed it had not yet received the funds.[^7] Focus Taiwan characterised the
July announcement as a mutual intent to work toward an agreement.[^46]

Hsu also rejected the word most coverage uses. Asked about the $500 million,
she emphasised it should not be viewed simply as a prepayment but as a
multi-function strategic arrangement that also serves as a performance-assurance
mechanism.[^44] That matters for the accounting: a performance-assurance
instrument is not deferred revenue, and GlobalWafers' reported customer
prepayment balance was NT$20.8 billion at the end of Q2,[^6] with Hsu calling
that level the trough: "I think now is the bottom."[^7]

Now look one layer downstream, because it reframes the whole transaction.
Micron disclosed in June 2026 that it holds sixteen strategic customer
agreements with binding volume commitments — fourteen of which carry roughly
$100 billion of cumulative revenue at minimum price — and about $22 billion of
customer commitments, of which roughly $18 billion is cash deposits — which Micron's CFO classifies as *financing* cash flows,
returned to customers in the back half of the term, explicitly not affecting
free cash flow.[^18]

Hyperscalers post roughly $18 billion of cash to Micron under take-or-pay
terms. Micron forwards $500 million — under 3% of what it received — to its
wafer supplier. This is not a memory maker funding its supply chain out of
generosity or even out of scarcity panic; it is a memory maker intermediating
downstream capital and retaining the overwhelming majority of it. The capacity
risk is being pushed up the chain one layer at a time, and each layer keeps a
cut.

There is a hard lesson underneath, and it comes from the supplier side. SUMCO's
own chief financial officer describes what happens when a customer
disappoints: "because the customers have not fully taken the volume stipulated
in the LTAs, the endpoint of the LTAs is being extended," and management
conceded it needs "to find a better way of dealing with such
circumstances."[^11] When a buyer underlifts, in other words, the seller
extends the term rather than enforcing the volume. ==An eight-to-ten-year LTA
is therefore not an enforceable claim on future revenue.== ==unverified: the
often-cited 2019 precedent, in which wafer suppliers cut contracted volumes to
defend contracted prices, rests on trade-press accounts I could not confirm
against a supplier filing.== The seller concedes timing when demand turns. Which means the
lengthening of contracts reads at least as naturally as a financing
arrangement — and as a *liability* in the wrong cycle — as it does as evidence
of scarcity.

## 07. The 2027 reset the bull case needs

Every bullish case here reduces to one proposition: the trough-priced contracts
signed in 2023–25 expire, and the industry re-signs at 2026 spot. Hsu is explicit: "For the meaningful
growth, revenue growth definitely will be from 2027."[^7] So how much of the book is actually repriceable?

No supplier will say. Siltronic confirms roughly two-thirds of sales sit under
LTAs, mostly 300mm, with durations "far above three years" and some running to
2030; asked point-blank by an analyst what portion expires in 2027 and 2028,
management disclosed only the two-thirds figure and said "We do not see an LTA
cliff or major LTA changes."[^9] GlobalWafers' spokesperson stated flatly: "we
are unable to disclose LTA specific coverage ratio," while noting that H2 2026 and
possibly H1 2027 revenue "will be basically coming from LTA," with "quite
certain percent" still arriving from spot.[^7] SUMCO's president
says only that the vast majority of its 300mm business is under LTAs.[^11]

Siltronic Q2 2026 call;[^9] GlobalWafers Q2 2026 call;[^7] SUMCO Q1 FY2026
call.[^11] The two short bars denote undisclosed figures, not small ones.

Then comes the finding that inverts the thesis. SUMCO's CFO explained in May
2026 that "because the customers have not fully taken the volume stipulated in
the LTAs, the endpoint of the LTAs is being extended," adding that "generally
speaking, the LTA periods are still getting longer."[^11] The contract book is
not maturing into a repricing event. It is *lengthening*, because underlifted
volume pushes the end date out. The same mechanism that produced longer
contracts — customers taking less than they committed to during the downturn —
is what defers the reset.

Put those pieces together and the repriceable slice in 2027 is roughly
Siltronic's non-LTA third, GlobalWafers' three-to-six-month spot RFQ book, and
a SUMCO spot book management does not consider large. And per Heckmeier, spot
still prices *below* LTA.[^9] Repricing the free slice at today's spot is a
markdown, not a windfall.

The measured cost of this lag is stark. ==SUMCO's operating margin fell every
year through the AI boom — 24.9% in FY2022, then 17.2%, 9.3% and 0.3% in
FY2025; that series is assembled from successive annual results rather than one
filing page.== Its customer SK hynix, meanwhile, reported a 76.3% operating
margin in calendar Q2 2026.[^50] That spread is the contract book, made visible. Some of it is peak
depreciation rather than pure pricing, so it overstates the pricing effect. But
the direction is not in doubt.

Meanwhile the demand-side cycle that would justify a 2027 reset is decelerating
in rate of change even as levels make new highs. TrendForce reports conventional
DRAM contract prices rising 93–98% quarter on quarter in 1Q26 and 58–63%
in 2Q26,[^54] and a forecast 13–18% in 3Q26 — roughly a sixfold deceleration in three
quarters.[^41] {sparkline:95,60,15} Decelerating is not falling. But a wafer
supplier signing a ten-year contract in late 2026 is pricing against the
strongest memory quarter in history, on a book it cannot reprice for a decade.

**The falsifiable checkpoint is close.** Hsu said in August that GlobalWafers
had only just begun signing this cycle's contracts and could give a firmer
answer in about six months,[^1] and Siltronic expects to relaunch its smaller
agreements "under new terms in 2027 and 2028."[^9] ==unverified: trade
reporting places the 2027 contract round in September–October 2026.== Either
way, the autumn negotiating season is the first moment the shortage reaches the
contract book or fails to.

## 08. China, policy, and the slower ways this ends

Two structural forces sit outside the price mechanism, and they run in opposite
directions.

**Chinese supply is large in nameplate and small where it matters.** Mainland
300mm nominal installed capacity was around 3 million wafers a month at
end-2024 with roughly another 1 million under construction — the issuer's own
filing puts "nominal" in scare quotes.[^33] ==Actual shipments are far lower: ESWIN shipped roughly 670,000 wafers a month
in 2025 against about 850,000 of nameplate, and National Silicon Industry Group
roughly 535,000 against a comparable nameplate — figures assembled from Chinese
filings that resisted direct verification.==[^33,34]

The decisive constraint is qualification tier, and here the primary evidence is
unusually clean because it is self-disclosed against interest. ESWIN — mainland
China's largest 300mm maker and the world's sixth — told the Shanghai Stock
Exchange that its products have not yet been applied to the world's most
advanced chip process nodes, and that its epitaxial wafers reach mass
production only at 28nm and above.[^32,33]
Its named volume customers are mature and specialty foundries.

The economics say the same thing. In 2025 NSIG grew 300mm volume about 27% to
roughly 6.4 million wafers while its 300mm average selling price fell close to
9% and the segment ran a **negative gross margin of about 15%**.[^34] In Q1 2026 it grew 300mm volume
more than 90% year on year with prices still falling and its net loss more than
doubling.[^35] That is capacity being pushed into the market below cash cost —
the behaviour of a subsidised entrant, not a supplier with pricing power. And ESWIN's
own filings carry the risk factor that if planned domestic and overseas
capacity ramps on schedule, global 12-inch silicon wafers may enter a *phased*
oversupply during 2026.[^32]

The widely cited Beijing target of over 70% domestic sourcing of advanced
silicon wafers by 2026 traces to a single Nikkei exclusive citing unnamed
sources, with no ministry document or published Chinese text behind it.[^36]
Treat it as reported intent, not binding policy. On the tier that matters —
leading-edge-qualified prime wafers — mainland share remains in the low single
digits.

**Policy is close to neutral on physical supply.** Allied export controls do
not reach bare polished or epitaxial 300mm silicon wafers as a commodity. The
control lists bind device-fabrication equipment, advanced logic and memory
chips, and compound or hetero-epitaxial substrates — silicon appears in the
substrate entries only as a layer inside a stacked multilayer structure, and
the standalone ingot and boule list omits it entirely.[^58]
China's countermeasure package — gallium, germanium, antimony, graphite, rare
earths — contains no silicon-chain item. The one exception worth noting is that
silicon epitaxial reactors do fall inside Japan's 2023 equipment list, and epi
is a separately constrained capacity pool.

The US incentive layer, by contrast, has a hard deadline. The Advanced
Manufacturing Investment Credit rate rose from 25% to 35% for property placed
in service after 31 December 2025,[^28] and Treasury's final regulations define
qualifying wafer production to reach all the way back to "growing single-crystal
ingots and boules, wafer slicing, etching and polishing, bonding, cleaning,
epitaxial deposition, and metrology" while excluding precursor materials such
as polysilicon.[^29] Wafer makers qualify — GlobalWafers reported approximately
$317.8 million received as of Q1 2026 under AMIC and other incentive programmes,
which is separate from and larger than its CHIPS grant receipts.[^27] But the
credit does not apply to property whose construction *begins* after 31 December
2026.[^28] Sherman phase 2 sits directly against that cliff.

One ownership change closes the picture: Doosan agreed on 31 July 2026 to buy
70.61% of SK Siltron from SK Inc. for ₩2.3 trillion, closing targeted for
January 2027.[^42] It is a transfer, not a combination — concentration in the
five-firm oligopoly is unchanged — but it moves the world's number-three 12-inch
supplier away from an owner with a captive memory customer.

## 09. What would falsify this — and what survives

This article's thesis is that the tightness is real in physics and contracts but
absent from price, and that LTA lengthening is better read as risk transfer than
as scarcity. Here is what would break it.

**If 2027 LTA prices settle up double digits this autumn, I am wrong on the
central point.** The negotiations reportedly open in September–October 2026.
GlobalWafers has said new contracts will price above current levels to reflect
inflation, depreciation, energy and tighter specifications.[^1] ==unverified: sell-side targets on SUMCO were raised sharply through mid-2026
on precisely this expectation, reportedly from ¥2,270 to ¥4,100 in May and
¥6,200 in July; I could not obtain the notes, and press summaries suggest the
second raise came from rolling the valuation base year forward rather than from
a published price assumption.==

**If Shin-Etsu is right and the others are lagging indicators, the shortage is
simply earlier than the reported numbers.** The market leader says orders exceed
capacity across many items.[^47] Shin-Etsu is the largest, best-capitalised and
most leading-edge-weighted supplier; it would see genuine scarcity first. The
counterargument is that its segment disclosure bundles non-wafer products, so
its margin cannot be read as a wafer price signal.

**If depreciation is the entire story, then 2027–28 mechanically fixes the
income statements without any price increase at all.** SUMCO's depreciation is
still rising — ¥33.6 billion in Q2, guided to ¥35.4 billion in Q3 — against
half-year capex of ¥19.4 billion.[^12] Siltronic left its full-year depreciation,
EBIT and capex expectations explicitly "unchanged" alongside a half year in
which depreciation alone nearly doubled.[^8] When that wave rolls
off, margins recover regardless. This is the most likely path, and it is neither
bullish nor bearish about silicon scarcity — it is an accounting event that will
be widely misread as one.

**What survives every attack:** the demand mechanism. No line of inquiry
produced evidence that AI or DRAM capex is being trimmed or that memory
inventory is building. HBM's wafer-per-bit intensity is disclosed by the
manufacturer,[^16] the failure of node migration to offset it is disclosed by
the manufacturer,[^17] and the interposer's wafer appetite is disclosed by the
foundry.[^20] The sharpest available caution is about timing rather than level:
the largest buyers have declined to put a 2027 capital-spending number on paper
at all, which is silence about the shape of the decade's second half rather
than a signal of retreat.

**Red-team pass: 3/3 top claims unbroken.** An adversarial review targeting the
three load-bearing claims — that eight-year LTAs predate 2026, that spot still
prices below contract, and that SEMI's 2025 data shows volume up while revenue
fell — found no contradicting source for any of them after independent
searching. The 2021 eight-year figure gained *additional* corroboration: a
separate August 2021 Taiwanese report gives the tenor range as 「短則三年，長則
八年」 — three years at the shortest, eight at the longest — which means the
three-to-eight band was already published five years before this cycle's
headline used it as news.

**What also survives: the supply lag.** Forty-two months from groundbreaking to
qualified customer supply at Sherman,[^22] six-to-nine-month per-customer
qualification, no diameter step function, and a top-two supplier that cut its
subsidised expansion by 74% into the upcycle.[^30] Even if the shortage is fully
real, nothing can be built fast enough to relieve it before 2028.

:::callout(kind=danger, label="The asymmetry")
Both halves of the bear case are supply-side *bullish*. Suppliers are not
building because prices are below reinvestment level; prices are below
reinvestment level because the contract book is full of 2023–25 pricing. That
is a mechanism for a *late, violent* price move rather than no price move —
which is exactly the shape of 2016 to 2018, when area shipments rose 18.6% and
revenue rose 58%.[^59] The disagreement in this article is about timing and about who
captures the move, not about whether silicon is structurally scarce.
:::

Sell-side framing after the Micron announcement read the deal as evidence that
wafer supply is the next AI bottleneck after packaging and HBM.[^48] The market
has already voted twice. GlobalWafers went limit-up to a record
NT$1,485 on 13 July 2026 on the Micron announcement,[^38] and closed at NT$872
on 6 August 2026 — roughly 41% lower — after a fire at Novara and a
half-year report showing margin compression.[^37,39] Some of that round trip is
a genuine operating shock: the late-July fire damaged part of the 8-inch
back-end process area and left the separate 12-inch line untouched,[^57] and led Hsu to guide 2026
revenue to "flat or slightly higher than last year… because of the fire.
Otherwise, we are supposed to have much higher revenue."[^39] But a 41%
drawdown on a plant fire in a non-core diameter is not primarily a fire story.
It is the market discovering that the shortage it priced in July has an
income-statement arrival date somewhere in 2027, and that nobody — not the
company, not its two largest competitors — will say how much of the book can be
repriced when it gets there.
