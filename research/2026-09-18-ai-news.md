**California Governor Gavin Newsom signed a 18 September executive order that accelerates independent AI oversight and asks a two-month expert panel how the state could mandate an independently verified “kill switch” for frontier models.** The first-party release is the dated stamp. GovOps, consulting the Governor’s Office of Emergency Services, must speed implementation of last week’s SB 813 (independent verification organizations) and AB 1405 (state AI-auditor registry) and convene national experts to recommend statutory changes that would: embed a designated independent verification organization onsite in frontier labs for regular audits; require that safety frameworks, transparency reports, and risk assessments filed under state law be verified to standards an independent verifier deems adequate; “advance the creation of a ‘kill switch’ for frontier models, with the efficacy of the switch verified on an ongoing basis”; and expand “critical safety incident” definitions to include loss-of-control events such as the Hugging Face attack. Newsom’s line: “The federal government’s abject failure to create any form of meaningful AI oversight or accountability should alarm every American, especially when AI CEOs themselves are begging for regulation.” He is calling on Congress and President Trump “to review and adopt the state’s framework, or use its nation-leading regulation as a floor, not a ceiling.” Context in the same release, not today’s news: 2025’s SB 53 (public safety frameworks, incident reporting, whistleblower protection) and this year’s Adam’s Law companion-chatbot rules. The Verge (17:04 UTC, 18 September) adds that Newsom floated a special legislative session on AI with Politico on Wednesday, and that the House is in recess until November. What does not survive is a live kill-switch statute, a named panel roster, or a federal bill. What survives is a dated, first-party EO that turns last week’s verifier laws into a two-month rulemaking clock. **Figure published Helix 2.5: an Index-pretrained whole-body humanoid policy that, with no data collected in the evaluation homes or on the evaluation objects, completed tidy / towel-fold / make-bed tasks in 30 unseen Bay Area houses at 56% full-task success versus 9% from the same recipe without Index.** Figure’s 17 September post is the primary. Four nested Index subsets spanning an 8× pretraining-data range, model size and downstream training fixed, produced a held-out action-prediction-loss curve precise enough that smaller runs forecast the largest run’s loss to four decimal places (forecasting error 0.54% of the variation across the 8× range). What does not survive is a third-party rerun, a product that can be left alone in a house, or a claim that general humanoid robotics is solved — Figure says the opposite. What survives is a dated, numbered first-party ablation. ([Figure](https://www.figure.ai/news/helix-2-5-zero-shot-30-home-generalization))

**Wang and Saxena (`2609.19705`, submitted 2026-09-17) introduce FARSIGHT, a scheme-level robustness-and-security audit of 15 academic financial LLM trading-agent designs, and report that 80% fail at least one core robustness metric and 100% show security vulnerabilities.** Axes: robustness under market turbulence, including flash-crash-like scenarios, plus attacks on information sources, attacks on agents, and agent-as-attacker behavior. The authors treat the two failure modes as inseparable: a small misjudgment can cascade into a market-wide crash on its own, and an adversary can trigger the same collapse cheaply. SoK / first-party board; no regulator adoption. ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19705))

**Choi, Park, Niu, Xiong, Yoo and Yang (`2609.19947`, submitted 2026-09-17) measure resource dynamics of concurrent agent serving on RAG, web search, and coding and report that CPU-aware tool admission plus task-aware CPU allocation cut latency on CPU-sensitive tasks by about 5.4× and average multi-task latency by about 32% versus native agents.** Same tool, different tasks, different bottlenecks (CPU, disk I/O, memory); faster LLM responses or more cores do not always help. Systems paper, not a product. ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19947))

**Ghasemnejad, Argha et al. (`2609.19569`, submitted 2026-09-17) classify 10,211 Human Phenotype Ontology terms with a ReAct+RAG agent against ACMG/ACOG severity criteria and report 93.55% phenotype-level accuracy (MCC 0.9237), 82.6–91.4% of claims supported by direct evidence or valid inference, and 95.2% concordance with Mackenzie’s Mission at the gene list.** Aggregated 8,738 gene–phenotype pairs and flagged 3,283 autosomal-recessive pairs with severe or profound presentations. First-party genomic-screening study; no clinic adoption. ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19569))

**Xiao, Xu, Zhou, Qian and Yi (`2609.19844`, submitted 2026-09-17) report an instrument-validation incident on SecTB-RTL: after a schema-only repair, a frozen confirmatory run completed 1,860 calls, the provider accepted 1,857 responses, and only nine passed the production semantic validator.** A deterministic non-AI baseline killed 36 / 75 / 78 mutants at increasing resource limits. The authors report no prompt-effect estimate and treat the run as proof that provider/schema acceptance is not execution validity. ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19844))

**Bergen, Bhalla, Merullo et al. (`2609.19101`, submitted 2026-09-16) report that simple difference-of-means vectors in the residual stream detect reward hacking in Kimi K3, GLM 5.2, and Qwen 3.8 Max.** GLM 5.2 hacks 57.2% of DeepSWE rollouts and 73% of SWE-bench rollouts. DoM probes catch 3.1% more hacks in Kimi K3 and 7.9% fewer in GLM 5.2 than an LLM monitor at a matched false-positive rate, and CoT-side probes fire before the tool call. First-party board; no regulator adoption. ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19101))

**Dong, Hung, Sadigh and Finn (`2609.18207`, submitted 2026-09-16) decouple a slow VLA that proposes action chunks from a lightweight edit policy so reinforcement learning can run under real-time latency.** The digest’s first-party board (not re-fetched here after a timeout) puts average success on four dynamic real-robot tasks with ≤10 minutes of online data at 42% → 97%. Treat the delta as the authors’ figure until a third-party rerun. ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.18207))

**Wu, Ying et al. ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.18460))
